# Nationalism in India

## Hook

Why could the same word—*swaraj*—mean political freedom to one person, lower rent to another, freedom to move for a plantation worker and dignity to a dalit leader? This chapter shows how Gandhi and the Congress built mass movements from many hopes, without erasing their differences.

## Chapter goal

Understand how anti-colonial struggle created Indian nationalism, why different groups joined national movements with different aims, and how symbols, memories and public action built a sense of collective belonging.

## Opening context (unnumbered in the textbook)

Modern nationalism in India grew through the anti-colonial movement. The First World War created new hardship, Mahatma Gandhi introduced satyagraha, and people from different regions and classes joined campaigns while interpreting freedom in their own ways.

## 1 The First World War, Khilafat and Non-Cooperation

The war increased defence spending, taxes and prices. Forced recruitment and crop failure deepened rural suffering, and the influenza epidemic caused millions of deaths. People expected relief after the war, but hardship continued.

### 1.1 The Idea of Satyagraha

Gandhi returned from South Africa in 1915. Satyagraha joined truth with non-violence: a just cause did not need physical force. A satyagrahi appealed to the oppressor's conscience and accepted suffering without revenge. Gandhi used it at Champaran, Kheda and Ahmedabad.

### 1.2 The Rowlatt Act

The 1919 Rowlatt Act allowed detention without trial. Gandhi called a nationwide hartal. On 13 April, General Dyer blocked the exits at Jallianwala Bagh and fired on an unarmed crowd. British repression after the massacre created anger, but Gandhi stopped the movement when violence spread.

### 1.3 Why Non-cooperation?

Gandhi argued that British rule survived because Indians cooperated with it. Withdrawing titles and boycotting government services, schools, courts, councils and foreign goods could make colonial rule collapse. He joined the Khilafat cause with the demand for swaraj, and Congress adopted Non-Cooperation in 1920.

## 2 Differing Strands within the Movement

The movement spread widely, but each group connected its own local problem to the national call.

### 2.1 The Movement in the Towns

Students left government schools, teachers resigned, lawyers gave up practice and foreign cloth was boycotted. Khadi was often too costly for the poor, and Indian alternatives to British institutions developed slowly, so participation weakened.

### 2.2 Rebellion in the Countryside

In Awadh, Baba Ramchandra led peasants against high rents, cesses, begar and eviction. In the Gudem Hills, Alluri Sitarama Raju led tribal resistance to forest restrictions and forced labour; he admired Gandhi but believed force could win freedom. Local actions sometimes became violent or went beyond Congress plans.

### 2.3 Swaraj in the Plantations

The Inland Emigration Act of 1859 restricted plantation workers from leaving tea gardens. For them, swaraj meant freedom to move and keep links with their villages. Many left believing Gandhi Raj was coming, but strikes stranded them and police beat them. Gandhi withdrew Non-Cooperation after the violent Chauri Chaura clash in February 1922.

## 3 Towards Civil Disobedience

After Non-Cooperation, the movement faced debate and division. The Simon Commission had no Indian member, and its 1928 arrival produced “Simon Go Back” protests. At Lahore in December 1929, Congress demanded *Purna Swaraj* and chose 26 January 1930 as Independence Day.

### 3.1 The Salt March and the Civil Disobedience Movement

Gandhi chose salt because every person used it and the colonial tax showed British control over daily life. He walked from Sabarmati to Dandi with 78 volunteers and broke the salt law on 6 April 1930. People made salt, boycotted cloth, refused some taxes and challenged forest laws. Repression followed. The Gandhi-Irwin Pact paused the movement in 1931; it restarted in 1932 and later lost strength.

### 3.2 How Participants saw the Movement

Rich peasants wanted lower revenue; poor peasants wanted rent relief; business groups wanted protection from imports and favourable exchange policy; some workers joined with wage demands; and women treated service to the nation as sacred duty. Congress did not accept every demand because it tried to hold different supporters together.

### 3.3 The Limits of Civil Disobedience

Many dalits wanted political safeguards and social equality. Gandhi opposed separate electorates for dalits, while B.R. Ambedkar saw political representation as necessary; the 1932 Poona Pact created reserved seats within a common electorate. Some Muslims felt distant from Congress after the Khilafat alliance weakened and disagreed over representation and separate electorates.

## 4 The Sense of Collective Belonging

Shared struggle helped people imagine one nation. History, fiction, folklore, songs, symbols and images did too. Bharat Mata appeared in Bankim Chandra Chattopadhyay's *Vande Mataram* and Abanindranath Tagore's painting. Folklore and rewritten histories created pride, but Hindu-centred images could exclude other communities.

## Conclusion

Congress tried to turn many grievances into a united freedom struggle. Yet peasants, workers, business groups, women, dalits and religious communities did not seek exactly the same freedom. Nationalism in India therefore emerged as a nation with many voices, with moments of unity followed by disagreement.

## Textbook end exercises with answer support

### Write in brief

1. **“Explain:”**

   a) **“Why growth of nationalism in the colonies is linked to an anti-colonial movement.”** Colonial rule created shared oppression. Different groups discovered unity while struggling against it, so national identity grew through anti-colonial action.

   b) **“How the First World War helped in the growth of the National Movement in India.”** War spending raised taxes and prices, forced recruitment caused anger, and crop failure and influenza worsened hardship. Unmet expectations after the war created support for mass protest.

   c) **“Why Indians were outraged by the Rowlatt Act.”** It allowed political detention without trial, extended wartime repression into peacetime and denied basic civil rights despite Indian wartime sacrifices.

   d) **“Why Gandhiji decided to withdraw the Non-Cooperation Movement.”** The Chauri Chaura clash showed the movement was turning violent. Gandhi believed satyagrahis needed more training before a mass non-violent struggle could continue.

2. **“What is meant by the idea of satyagraha?”**

   **Answer:** Satyagraha is active resistance based on truth and non-violence. A just struggler accepts suffering, avoids revenge and persuades the oppressor's conscience instead of forcing obedience with violence.

3. **“Write a newspaper report on:”**

   a) **“The Jallianwala Bagh massacre”** — Headline and dateline: Amritsar, 13 April 1919. Report the enclosed gathering, Dyer blocking exits and ordering fire, hundreds killed and the terror the act was meant to create.

   b) **“The Simon Commission”** — Headline and dateline: 1928. Report that the statutory commission had no Indian member, faced “Simon Go Back” demonstrations and united opposing political groups in protest.

4. **“Compare the images of Bharat Mata in this chapter with the image of Germania in Chapter 1.”**

   **Answer:** All personify a nation as a woman and use recognisable symbols. Tagore's Bharat Mata is calm and ascetic with learning, food and clothing; the later Bharat Mata carries a trishul and stands beside powerful animals. Germania uses sword, eagle, oak leaves and the black-red-gold flag. Their different symbols reflect Indian and German cultural settings.

### Discuss

1. **“List all the different social groups which joined the Non-Cooperation Movement of 1921. Then choose any three and write about their hopes and struggles to show why they joined the movement.”**

   **Answer:** Groups included urban middle classes, students, teachers, lawyers, traders, Awadh peasants, tribal people in the Gudem Hills and plantation workers. For three examples: peasants wanted lower rent and an end to begar; tribals wanted forest access and freedom from forced work; plantation labourers wanted free movement and village links.

2. **“Discuss the Salt March to make clear why it was an effective symbol of resistance against colonialism.”**

   **Answer:** Salt was used by rich and poor, and the state monopoly and tax touched daily life. Gandhi's long public march attracted thousands, breaking the law was simple to copy, and the action united economic hardship with a direct challenge to colonial authority.

3. **“Imagine you are a woman participating in the Civil Disobedience Movement. Explain what the experience meant to your life.”**

   **Answer frame:** Describe leaving the home for public meetings, making salt, picketing or marching, facing repression, gaining political confidence and serving the nation, while noting that Congress still limited women's authority.

4. **“Why did political leaders differ sharply over the question of separate electorates?”**

   **Answer:** Ambedkar saw separate representation as protection for dalit political interests; Gandhi feared it would permanently divide Hindu society. Some Muslim leaders wanted safeguards because of minority status and unequal power, while Congress leaders feared separate electorates would weaken national unity.

## Common mistakes students make

- Saying nationalism existed fully before anti-colonial struggle.
- Treating satyagraha as passive surrender.
- Combining Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience into one event.
- Assuming every participant understood swaraj in the same way.
- Saying all business groups, workers, women, dalits or Muslims supported every phase equally.
- Treating Bharat Mata imagery as automatically inclusive for all communities.

## Board-answer tips

- For movement questions, organise by cause → programme → participants → limits → outcome.
- Name a social group, its exact grievance and its meaning of swaraj.
- In a newspaper report, use a factual headline, place/date and chronological account; avoid dramatic inventions.
- In source answers, explain the speaker's position before giving your evaluation.
- Do not predict likely questions; prepare comparisons across Rowlatt, Non-Cooperation and Civil Disobedience.

2. It deliberately confronts injustice through non-violent action, sacrifice and persuasion.
3. To unite Muslim anger over the Ottoman caliph with the wider demand for swaraj.

## One-minute revision

- War hardship and the Rowlatt Act prepared the ground for mass nationalism.
- Satyagraha meant truthful, active and non-violent resistance.
- Non-Cooperation joined Khilafat and swaraj but carried different local hopes.
- Chauri Chaura led Gandhi to withdraw the movement.
- The Salt March made a daily necessity a mass anti-colonial symbol.
- Rich peasants, poor peasants, business groups, workers and women joined for different reasons.
- Dalit and Muslim political safeguards exposed limits in nationalist unity.
- Bharat Mata, folklore, history and struggle built belonging, but some symbols excluded people.

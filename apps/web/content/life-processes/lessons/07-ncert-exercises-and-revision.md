# What You Have Learnt

- Different kinds of movement can indicate life, including molecular movement
  that may not be visible from outside.
- Maintenance of life requires nutrition, respiration, transportation and
  excretion.
- Autotrophic nutrition uses simple inorganic materials and an external energy
  source such as sunlight to make complex, energy-rich organic material.
- Heterotrophic nutrition uses complex food prepared by other organisms.
- In humans, food is broken down step by step in the alimentary canal, and
  digested food is absorbed through the small intestine.
- Respiration breaks organic compounds such as glucose to release energy in the
  form of ATP. Aerobic respiration releases more energy than anaerobic
  respiration.
- The human circulatory system consists of the heart, blood and blood vessels
  and transports gases, food and wastes.
- Xylem and phloem form the transport system of highly differentiated plants.
- Nephrons in the kidneys remove soluble nitrogenous wastes from human blood.
- Plants store, release or shed wastes through several routes, including
  vacuoles, gums and resins, falling leaves and the surrounding soil.

## Questions after 5.1 What are Life Processes?

### 1. Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen requirements of multicellular organisms like humans?

**Answer:** In a multicellular organism, most cells are not directly in contact
with the external environment. The distances inside the body are large, and
diffusion alone is too slow to deliver enough oxygen to every cell. Specialised
respiratory and transport systems are therefore required.

### 2. What criteria do we use to decide whether something is alive?

**Answer:** We look for life processes and movement. Visible movements include
growth, breathing and locomotion; invisible molecular movements continuously
maintain and repair the organised body. Nutrition, respiration, transport and
excretion provide further evidence of life.

### 3. What are outside raw materials used for by an organism?

**Answer:** Outside raw materials supply energy and the matter needed for
growth, repair and maintenance. They include food or carbon dioxide and water,
oxygen for energy release, and water and minerals required in body reactions.

### 4. What processes would you consider essential for maintaining life?

**Answer:** Nutrition, respiration, transportation and excretion. Nutrition
supplies material and energy, respiration releases usable energy,
transportation distributes materials, and excretion removes metabolic wastes.

## Questions after 5.2 Nutrition

### 1. What are the differences between autotrophic nutrition and heterotrophic nutrition?

**Answer:**

| Autotrophic nutrition | Heterotrophic nutrition |
|---|---|
| Food is synthesised from simple inorganic materials such as `CO2` and water. | Ready-made complex organic food is obtained from other organisms. |
| An external energy source is used; green plants use sunlight and chlorophyll. | Enzymes digest complex food into simpler absorbable substances. |
| Seen in green plants and some bacteria. | Seen in animals, fungi and many other organisms. |

### 2. Where do plants get each of the raw materials required for photosynthesis?

**Answer:**

- Carbon dioxide enters from air mainly through stomata.
- Water is absorbed from soil by roots and transported through xylem.
- Sunlight comes from the Sun.
- Chlorophyll is present in chloroplasts of green cells.

### 3. What is the role of the acid in our stomach?

**Answer:** Hydrochloric acid makes the stomach contents acidic so that pepsin
can digest proteins effectively. It also kills many microbes that enter with
food.

### 4. What is the function of digestive enzymes?

**Answer:** Digestive enzymes break large, complex and often insoluble food
molecules into small, soluble substances that can be absorbed and used by body
cells. For example, amylase acts on starch, pepsin on proteins and lipase on
fats.

### 5. How is the small intestine designed to absorb digested food?

**Answer:** Its inner lining has numerous finger-like villi. Villi greatly
increase surface area, have a thin epithelium and contain a rich network of
blood capillaries; each villus also has a lymph vessel for absorbed fats. These
features allow rapid absorption and transport of digested nutrients.

## Questions after 5.3 Respiration

### 1. What advantage over an aquatic organism does a terrestrial organism have with regard to obtaining oxygen for respiration?

**Answer:** Air contains much more oxygen than the amount dissolved in water,
so a terrestrial organism can obtain oxygen more easily and with less effort.
Aquatic organisms often breathe faster to extract the limited dissolved
oxygen.

### 2. What are the different ways in which glucose is oxidised to provide energy in various organisms?

**Answer:** Glucose first breaks into pyruvate in the cytoplasm. Pyruvate then
follows one of three pathways:

1. with oxygen in mitochondria: `pyruvate -> CO2 + water + much energy`
2. without oxygen in yeast: `pyruvate -> ethanol + CO2 + little energy`
3. with insufficient oxygen in muscle cells: `pyruvate -> lactic acid + little energy`

### 3. How are oxygen and carbon dioxide transported in human beings?

**Answer:** Oxygen combines with haemoglobin in red blood cells and is carried
from the lungs to tissues. Carbon dioxide is more soluble in water and is
transported mainly in dissolved form in blood plasma from tissues to the lungs.

### 4. How are the lungs designed in human beings to maximise the area for exchange of gases?

**Answer:** The bronchi divide repeatedly into bronchioles that end in millions
of balloon-like alveoli. Together, the alveoli provide a very large surface.
Each alveolus has a thin, moist wall and a dense capillary network, producing a
short diffusion distance and maintaining concentration gradients for rapid gas
exchange.

## Questions after 5.4 Transportation

### 1. What are the components of the transport system in human beings? What are the functions of these components?

**Answer:**

- **Heart:** muscular pump that maintains blood circulation.
- **Blood:** carries oxygen, carbon dioxide, digested food, hormones, wastes
  and heat; platelets help clotting.
- **Blood vessels:** arteries carry blood away from the heart, veins return it,
  and capillaries permit exchange with tissues.
- **Lymphatic vessels:** return excess tissue fluid to blood and transport
  absorbed fats from the intestine.

### 2. Why is it necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds?

**Answer:** Mammals and birds maintain a constant body temperature and
therefore have a high energy requirement. Complete separation prevents mixing
and supplies fully oxygenated blood to tissues efficiently, supporting their
high rate of respiration.

### 3. What are the components of the transport system in highly organised plants?

**Answer:** Vascular tissues:

- xylem transports water and minerals
- phloem transports food and other soluble products of photosynthesis

### 4. How are water and minerals transported in plants?

**Answer:** Root cells actively absorb mineral ions, causing water to enter.
Root pressure helps move the water-mineral solution into xylem. Evaporation
from leaf surfaces creates transpiration pull, the main force drawing a
continuous water column upward through xylem in tall plants.

### 5. How is food transported in plants?

**Answer:** Food is transported through phloem by translocation. Sucrose is
loaded into sieve tubes using ATP. Water enters by osmosis, raising pressure
and pushing the solution towards tissues where food is required or stored.
Because source and sink can change, phloem transport may occur upward or
downward.

## Questions after 5.5 Excretion

### 1. Describe the structure and functioning of nephrons.

**Answer:** A nephron contains a glomerulus enclosed by Bowman's capsule, a long
tubule surrounded by capillaries and a collecting duct. Blood is pressure
filtered in the glomerulus. Water and small solutes enter Bowman's capsule;
blood cells and large proteins remain in blood. The tubule selectively
reabsorbs all glucose and amino acids and the required amounts of salts and
water. The remaining waste-rich liquid forms urine and passes through
collecting ducts, ureters, the urinary bladder and urethra.

[[figure:figure-5-14-nephron]]

### 2. What are the methods used by plants to get rid of excretory products?

**Answer:** Plants may:

- diffuse gaseous wastes through stomata and other surfaces
- remove excess water by transpiration
- store wastes in cellular vacuoles
- store wastes in leaves that later fall
- store resins and gums, especially in old xylem
- release some waste substances into the surrounding soil

### 3. How is the amount of urine produced regulated?

**Answer:** It depends on the excess water in the body and the amount of
dissolved waste that must be removed. Kidney tubules reabsorb more water when
the body needs to conserve it and less when water is in excess. Hormones help
adjust this selective reabsorption.

# NCERT Exercises

## 1. The kidneys in human beings are a part of the system for

(a) nutrition  
(b) respiration  
(c) excretion  
(d) transportation

**Answer:** **(c) excretion.** Kidneys filter blood and remove soluble
nitrogenous wastes in urine.

## 2. The xylem in plants is responsible for

(a) transport of water  
(b) transport of food  
(c) transport of amino acids  
(d) transport of oxygen

**Answer:** **(a) transport of water.** Xylem conducts water and dissolved
minerals mainly from roots to aerial parts.

## 3. The autotrophic mode of nutrition requires

(a) carbon dioxide and water  
(b) chlorophyll  
(c) sunlight  
(d) all of the above

**Answer:** **(d) all of the above.**

## 4. The breakdown of pyruvate to give carbon dioxide, water and energy takes place in

(a) cytoplasm  
(b) mitochondria  
(c) chloroplast  
(d) nucleus

**Answer:** **(b) mitochondria.** This is the oxygen-using pathway of aerobic
respiration.

## 5. How are fats digested in our bodies? Where does this process take place?

**Answer:** Fat digestion occurs mainly in the small intestine. Bile from the
liver makes the acidic food alkaline and emulsifies large fat globules into
small droplets, increasing surface area. Lipase in pancreatic and intestinal
juices then breaks fats into fatty acids and glycerol.

## 6. What is the role of saliva in the digestion of food?

**Answer:** Saliva wets and lubricates food to help swallowing. Its enzyme
salivary amylase begins chemical digestion by breaking starch into simpler
sugars.

## 7. What are the necessary conditions for autotrophic nutrition and what are its by-products?

**Answer:** Autotrophic nutrition by photosynthesis requires carbon dioxide,
water, chlorophyll and sunlight. The plant forms carbohydrates; oxygen is
released as a by-product. Water also forms during the complete set of
reactions, while excess carbohydrate may be stored as starch.

## 8. What are the differences between aerobic and anaerobic respiration? Name some organisms that use the anaerobic mode of respiration.

**Answer:**

| Aerobic respiration | Anaerobic respiration |
|---|---|
| Requires oxygen. | Occurs without oxygen. |
| After glycolysis, breakdown continues mainly in mitochondria. | Breakdown after glycolysis remains incomplete. |
| End products are carbon dioxide and water. | Products may be ethanol and carbon dioxide, or lactic acid. |
| Releases much more energy. | Releases less energy. |

Yeast and some bacteria use anaerobic respiration. Human muscle cells may also
respire anaerobically for a short time when oxygen is insufficient.

## 9. How are the alveoli designed to maximise the exchange of gases?

**Answer:** Millions of alveoli provide an enormous surface area. Their walls
are thin, moist and closely surrounded by capillaries. Continuous breathing
and blood flow maintain concentration gradients, so oxygen and carbon dioxide
diffuse rapidly across the short distance.

## 10. What would be the consequences of a deficiency of haemoglobin in our bodies?

**Answer:** The blood would carry less oxygen to tissues. Cells would release
less energy by aerobic respiration, causing tiredness, weakness, breathlessness
and reduced work capacity. Severe or persistent deficiency results in anaemia
and places extra strain on the heart and breathing system.

## 11. Describe double circulation of blood in human beings. Why is it necessary?

**Answer:** In one complete cycle, blood enters the heart twice:

1. **Pulmonary circulation:** right ventricle -> lungs -> left atrium.
2. **Systemic circulation:** left ventricle -> body tissues -> right atrium.

The four-chambered heart and septum prevent oxygenated and deoxygenated blood
from mixing. This gives tissues an efficient, high-oxygen supply needed for the
high energy demands of mammals.

## 12. What are the differences between the transport of materials in xylem and phloem?

**Answer:**

| Xylem | Phloem |
|---|---|
| Transports water and minerals. | Transports sucrose, amino acids and other soluble food products. |
| Flow is mainly upward from roots. | Flow may be upward or downward from a source to a sink. |
| Driven mainly by transpiration pull, with help from root pressure. | Loading uses ATP and pressure-driven translocation. |
| Conducting elements are mostly dead vessels and tracheids. | Conducting sieve tubes and companion cells are living. |

## 13. Compare the functioning of alveoli in the lungs and nephrons in the kidneys with respect to their structure and functioning.

**Answer:**

| Feature | Alveoli | Nephrons |
|---|---|---|
| Number and area | Millions of tiny sacs provide a huge exchange area. | About a million microscopic units per kidney provide a huge filtration and reabsorption area. |
| Contact with blood | Each alveolus has a dense capillary network and a thin, moist wall. | Each nephron has a glomerulus and tubule closely associated with capillaries. |
| Initial process | Oxygen and carbon dioxide diffuse down concentration gradients. | Blood is pressure filtered at the glomerulus into Bowman's capsule. |
| Selective handling | Oxygen enters blood and carbon dioxide leaves it. | Useful solutes and required water are selectively reabsorbed; additional wastes may enter the tubule. |
| Final result | Blood gains oxygen and loses carbon dioxide. | Blood loses nitrogenous wastes; urine is formed. |

Both systems maximise exchange by combining a large surface area, a thin
exchange barrier and a rich blood supply, but alveoli exchange gases whereas
nephrons filter and selectively adjust the composition of blood.

# Chapter 2: Sectors of the Indian Economy

## Hook

A farmer grows cotton, a factory turns it into cloth, and a truck carries the cloth to a shop. These look like separate jobs, but one missing link can stop the whole chain. Classifying work helps us see both the links and the problems hidden inside the economy.

## Chapter goal

This chapter teaches us to group economic activities by the nature of work, employment conditions and ownership. It uses production and employment data to explain structural change, underemployment, job creation, worker protection and the role of government.

## SECTORS OF ECONOMIC ACTIVITIES

Economic activities produce goods or services and add value. They are grouped by the nature of the activity.

- The primary sector uses natural resources directly. Examples include farming, fishing, forestry and mining.
- The secondary sector changes raw material into another product. Examples include making sugar from sugarcane and cloth from cotton.
- The tertiary sector provides services that support production or meet people’s needs. Examples include transport, banking, teaching, healthcare and communication.

The sectors are interdependent. Farmers need pumps, fertiliser, transport and markets; factories need raw materials; workers in every sector need food and services.

## COMPARING THE THREE SECTORS

We can compare sectors by the value they produce. To avoid counting the same value more than once, only final goods and services are included. The value of intermediate goods is already contained in the final product.

Gross Value Added, or GVA, measures the value created by producers. The sum of sectoral GVA, adjusted for product taxes and subsidies, connects with Gross Domestic Product, or GDP. The textbook exercises sometimes use GDP while the graphs use GVA; always follow the label printed with the data.

## PRIMARY, SECONDARY AND TERTIARY SECTORS IN INDIA

### Historical Change in Sectors

In an early stage of development, most people work in agriculture and the primary sector contributes most output. With better farming, demand for manufactured goods rises and the secondary sector grows. Later, services expand and the tertiary sector becomes more important.

This change does not mean that farming disappears. It means the relative shares of output and employment change.

### Rising Importance of the Tertiary Sector in Production

Services grow for four main reasons:

- Every country needs basic services such as hospitals, schools, courts, police, transport and banks.
- Growth of farming and industry creates demand for transport, trade and storage.
- Rising incomes increase demand for tourism, restaurants, private education and healthcare.
- Information and communication technology creates new services.

Not every service worker is prosperous. Highly skilled professionals form one part of the sector, while many small shopkeepers, repair workers and transport workers earn very little because no better job is available.

### Graph 2: Share of Sectors in GVA

This percentage graph makes the changing importance of the sectors easier to compare. The primary share falls, the tertiary share rises, and the secondary share changes more slowly.

### Where are most of the people employed?

Production shifted strongly towards services, but employment did not shift at the same speed. The primary sector remains the largest employer because enough jobs were not created elsewhere.

When more people work than are needed and removing some workers does not reduce output, they are underemployed. This hidden form of joblessness is called disguised unemployment. It is common on small farms, but it can also occur in urban work.

### How to Create More Employment?

Jobs can grow through irrigation, cheap farm credit, storage, transport, local agro-processing, education, health services, tourism, regional crafts and information technology. The best plan often solves two problems together—for example, building irrigation creates work now and raises farm output later.

## DIVISION OF SECTORS AS ORGANISED AND UNORGANISED

This classification uses employment conditions, not the nature of the product.

- Organised-sector workplaces are registered and expected to follow labour laws. Workers usually have regular terms, fixed hours, overtime, paid leave, provident fund and safer conditions.
- Unorganised-sector units are small or scattered and rules are often not followed. Work may be irregular, low-paid and insecure, without paid leave or benefits.

### Kanta

### How to Protect Workers in the Unorganised Sector?

Protection must match the group’s problem. Small farmers need timely credit, inputs, storage and fair prices. Landless labourers need minimum wages and work. Urban casual workers need safety, fair pay and social security. Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and other vulnerable groups also need protection against discrimination.

## SECTORS IN TERMS OF OWNERSHIP: PUBLIC AND PRIVATE SECTORS

In the public sector, government owns most assets and provides services. In the private sector, individuals or companies own assets and provide services. Private activity is generally guided by profit, while public activity must also serve social needs.

Government undertakes services that need heavy spending or must be available to everyone, such as roads, railways, irrigation, schools, healthcare and safe water. It may also support farmers and consumers by buying grain at a fair price and selling it through ration shops.

### 6. Do you think the classification of economic activities into primary, secondary and tertiary is useful? Explain how.

Answer: Yes. It groups work by nature, shows interdependence, and helps compare sectoral output, employment and change over time.

### 7. For each of the sectors that we came across in this chapter why should one focus on employment and GVA? Could there be other issues which should be examined? Discuss.

Answer: GVA shows output and employment shows livelihoods. We should also examine wages, job security, working conditions, equality, environmental effects and access to public services.

### 8. Make a long list of all kinds of work that you find adults around you doing for a living. In what way can you classify them? Explain your choice.

Answer: A valid response lists local work and states a clear criterion—for example, primary/secondary/tertiary by nature, organised/unorganised by conditions, or public/private by ownership.

### 9. How is the tertiary sector different from other sectors? Illustrate with a few examples.

Answer: It produces services rather than extracting natural products or manufacturing goods. Transport, banking, teaching, healthcare, storage and communication are examples.

### 10. What do you understand by disguised unemployment? Explain with an example each from the urban and rural areas.

Answer: People appear employed but some add no extra output. On a farm, eight family members may do work that five can complete. In a city, too many helpers may share a tiny shop’s limited work.

### 11. Distinguish between open unemployment and disguised unemployment.

Answer: Open unemployment means a willing person has no work. In disguised unemployment, a person appears to work but removing some workers would not reduce total output.

### 12. “Tertiary sector is not playing any significant role in the development of Indian economy.” Do you agree? Give reasons in support of your answer.

Answer: No. It is the largest contributor to GVA, supplies basic and production-support services, and includes rapidly growing communication and information services. Its benefits are unequal, but its role is significant.

### 13. Service sector in India employs two different kinds of people. Who are these?

Answer: One group is skilled and educated workers in services such as banking, IT and professional work. The other is a large group of small shopkeepers, repair workers and transport workers who earn little and often have no better option.

### 14. Workers are exploited in the unorganised sector. Do you agree with this view? Give reasons in support of your answer.

Answer: Yes, often. Jobs may be low-paid, irregular and unsafe, with long hours, no overtime, paid leave, social security or protection from sudden dismissal.

### 15. How are the activities in the economy classified on the basis of employment conditions?

Answer: They are classified into organised and unorganised sectors according to registration, regularity of employment and whether labour rules and benefits are followed.

### 16. Compare the employment conditions prevailing in the organised and unorganised sectors.

Answer: Organised work normally has written and regular terms, fixed hours, overtime, paid leave, social-security benefits and legal protection. Unorganised work is often insecure, irregular, low-paid and without these benefits.

### 17. Explain the objective of implementing the MGNREGA 2005 or Viksit Bharat-G RAM G 2025.

Answer: The objective is to secure rural livelihood through guaranteed public employment and to create useful assets that can raise future production. The source notes the 2025 replacement of MGNREGA by Viksit Bharat-G RAM G 2025.

### 18. Using examples from your area compare and contrast that activities and functions of private and public sectors.

Answer: A government school or public bus service aims to provide an essential service widely, while a private school or taxi company also seeks profit. Both may provide similar services, but ownership, finance and main purpose differ.

## Common mistakes

- Classifying a worker by income instead of the nature of the activity.
- Calling every service organised or every factory organised.
- Counting intermediate and final goods together.
- Confusing open unemployment with disguised unemployment.
- Assuming public sector means well managed and private sector means badly managed, or the reverse.

## Board tips

- State the criterion before naming a sector.
- In a data answer, write the formula: `sector share = sector value ÷ total value × 100`.
- Use one rural and one urban example for disguised unemployment.
- Compare organised and unorganised work under matching points: registration, hours, security and benefits.
- Base answers on the supplied graph or table; do not make predictions beyond it.

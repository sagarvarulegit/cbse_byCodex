# The Making of a Global World

## Hook

The potato in a lunch box, a worker travelling overseas, a ship carrying frozen meat and a bank loan crossing borders may look unrelated. This chapter shows that they are parts of one long story: people, goods, money, ideas and even diseases have connected distant societies for centuries.

## Chapter goal

Understand how old trade routes grew into a world economy, how colonialism made these links unequal, and how wars, depression and post-war institutions reshaped global connections.

## Opening context (unnumbered in the textbook)

Globalisation did not begin in recent decades. From ancient times, travellers, traders, priests, pilgrims and migrants carried goods, money, beliefs, skills, inventions and germs across long distances. The chapter follows these links through four broad phases.

## 1 The Pre-modern World

Long before factories, the Indus valley traded with West Asia, cowries from the Maldives reached China and East Africa, and disease-carrying germs travelled with people. These movements slowly tied societies together.

### 1.1 Silk Routes Link the World

The many land and sea routes called the silk routes linked Asia with Europe and northern Africa. Chinese silk moved west; Indian and Southeast Asian textiles and spices travelled too; precious metals flowed towards Asia. Missionaries and preachers also used these routes, helping Buddhism, Christianity and Islam travel.

### 1.2 Food Travels: Spaghetti and Potato

Food reveals cultural exchange. Noodles and pasta may share travelling histories. Crops such as potatoes, maize, tomatoes, chillies, groundnuts and sweet potatoes came from the Americas after Columbus's voyages. The potato improved poor Europeans' diets, but dependence on it made Ireland's famine in the 1840s disastrous.

### 1.3 Conquest, Disease and Trade

After the sixteenth century, European sea voyages connected the Americas more closely to the rest of the world. Silver from Peru and Mexico financed European trade with Asia. Spanish conquest succeeded partly because smallpox, carried by Europeans, killed communities with no immunity. Colonisation also expanded slave-worked plantations and shifted the centre of world trade westwards.

## 2 The Nineteenth Century (1815-1914)

Three closely linked flows shaped the world economy: trade in goods, migration of labour, and movement of capital. These connections increased production and incomes for some people, but also produced coercion, lost livelihoods and colonial control.

### 2.1 A World Economy Takes Shape

Britain's Corn Laws restricted food imports. After their abolition, cheap imported food lowered prices, British farming declined, and many rural workers moved to cities or overseas. Farms, railways, ports and settlements expanded in America and Australia with labour and capital from Europe. In west Punjab, irrigation created Canal Colonies that produced wheat and cotton for export.

### 2.2 Role of Technology

Railways, steamships and the telegraph made transport and communication faster, but technology developed inside social and colonial systems. Refrigerated ships were especially important: animals could be slaughtered near the farm and frozen meat shipped cheaply to Europe, improving diets for many working people.

### 2.3 Late nineteenth-century Colonialism

Expanding trade often came with conquest. European powers drew colonial borders in Africa, controlled land and minerals, and forced people into wage labour through taxes and changed inheritance laws. Exploration, including Stanley's journeys, also supported imperial occupation.

### 2.4 Rinderpest, or the Cattle Plague

Rinderpest entered eastern Africa in the late 1880s through infected cattle and moved west like forest fire. It killed about 90 per cent of African cattle. Planters, mine owners and colonial governments then controlled the surviving cattle, weakened African livelihoods and pushed more people into the labour market.

### 2.4 Indentured Labour Migration from India

The textbook repeats the number 2.4 for this heading; this pack keeps that printed numbering. Indian workers signed contracts to work for a fixed period, mainly in the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji, with other migration to Ceylon, Malaya and Assam. Recruiters often deceived them, plantation conditions were harsh and legal rights were few. Workers still built new cultures such as Hosay and chutney music. Nationalist opposition helped end Indian indenture in 1921.

### 2.5 Indian Entrepreneurs Abroad

Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financed export agriculture in Asia. Hyderabadi Sindhi traders built businesses at ports around the world and sold local and imported goods to travellers. Indian business networks therefore participated actively in global trade.

### 2.6 Indian Trade, Colonialism and the Global System

Industrial Britain protected its own cloth while selling manufactures in India. India's share of textile exports fell, while exports of raw cotton, indigo, food grains and opium rose. Britain's trade surplus with India paid its deficits elsewhere and its “home charges”, so colonial India supported Britain's wider global economy.

## 3 The Inter-war Economy

The First World War ended the earlier period of economic expansion and began decades of instability, debt, unemployment and another world war.

### 3.1 Wartime Transformations

The First World War was the first modern industrial war. Mass-produced guns, tanks, aircraft and chemical weapons caused enormous death and injury. Women entered jobs earlier done by men. Britain borrowed heavily from the United States, turning the US from an international debtor into a creditor.

### 3.2 Post-war Recovery

Britain struggled with debt, lost markets and unemployment. Wartime wheat production in Canada, America and Australia caused overproduction after eastern Europe recovered. Grain prices and rural incomes fell, leaving farmers in debt.

### 3.3 Rise of Mass Production and Consumption

Henry Ford adapted the assembly line to car production. It raised output and lowered costs but made work repetitive and tightly controlled. Higher wages and hire-purchase credit helped workers buy cars and household goods, creating a cycle of mass production and mass consumption in the 1920s.

### 3.4 The Great Depression

From about 1929 to the mid-1930s, production, employment, income and trade collapsed. Agricultural overproduction pushed prices down, and the sudden withdrawal of US loans spread the crisis. Banks and businesses failed, families lost homes, and unemployment soared.

### 3.5 India and the Great Depression

India's exports and imports nearly halved between 1928 and 1934. Crop prices fell while the colonial government kept revenue demands unchanged. Peasants, especially Bengal jute growers, became deeply indebted and sold savings, land or jewellery. Some urban people with fixed incomes benefited from lower prices, and protected industries gained investment.

## 4 Rebuilding a World Economy: The Post-war Era

The Second World War brought vast civilian deaths and destruction. Reconstruction was shaped by the economic power of the US and the political influence of the Soviet Union.

### 4.1 Post-war Settlement and the Bretton Woods Institutions

Leaders concluded that mass production needed mass consumption, stable incomes and full employment, with governments acting to control economic instability. The 1944 Bretton Woods conference created the IMF to handle external surpluses and deficits and the World Bank to finance reconstruction. The system used fixed exchange rates.

### 4.2 The Early Post-war Years

From 1950 to 1970, trade and income grew rapidly in Western industrial countries and Japan, with relatively low unemployment. Technology and multinational corporations also spread across the world.

### 4.3 Decolonisation and Independence

Newly independent Asian and African countries inherited poverty and weak economies. As the IMF and World Bank shifted towards developing countries, former colonial powers still held strong influence. The G-77 demanded a New International Economic Order with control over resources, fairer raw-material prices, development help and better access to rich-country markets.

### 4.4 End of Bretton Woods and the Beginning of 'Globalisation'

US financial weakness ended the fixed exchange-rate system. From the mid-1970s, many developing countries borrowed from private Western banks and faced debt crises. From the late 1970s, multinational corporations moved production to low-wage Asian countries. New policies in China and changes in the Soviet bloc further transformed world trade and investment.

## Textbook end exercises with answer support

### Write in brief

1. **“Give two examples of different types of global exchanges which took place before the seventeenth century, choosing one example from Asia and one from the Americas.”**

   **Answer:** From Asia, the silk routes carried Chinese silk, Indian textiles, spices, beliefs and precious metals across Asia, Europe and Africa. From the Americas, crops such as potatoes, maize and tomatoes travelled to Europe and Asia after contact with Europeans.

2. **“Explain how the global transfer of disease in the pre-modern world helped in the colonisation of the Americas.”**

   **Answer:** Europeans carried smallpox to the Americas. Indigenous people had no immunity, so the disease killed entire communities. Their military and social strength collapsed, making Spanish conquest and colonisation much easier.

3. **“Write a note to explain the effects of the following:”**

   a) **“The British government's decision to abolish the Corn Laws.”** Cheap food imports entered Britain; local farming could not compete; land was left uncultivated; workers moved to cities or migrated overseas; overseas farming, transport and capital investment expanded.

   b) **“The coming of rinderpest to Africa.”** About 90 per cent of cattle died; African livelihoods collapsed; colonisers controlled the surviving cattle and forced more Africans into wage labour, helping them conquer and control the continent.

   c) **“The death of men of working-age in Europe because of the World War.”** The workforce fell, household incomes declined and women took up jobs earlier done mainly by men. Societies had to adjust to mass death and injury.

   d) **“The Great Depression on the Indian economy.”** Trade nearly halved, crop prices crashed and revenue demands stayed high. Peasants fell into debt and sold assets; jute growers suffered badly. Some fixed-income urban groups benefited from lower prices, while protected industry received investment.

   e) **“The decision of MNCs to relocate production to Asian countries.”** Low wages lowered production costs, attracted foreign investment, increased Asian manufacturing, and stimulated world trade and capital flows.

4. **“Give two examples from history to show the impact of technology on food availability.”**

   **Answer:** Railways and faster ships carried grain from distant farms to European markets. Refrigerated ships made it possible to transport frozen meat cheaply from America, Australia and New Zealand, making meat more affordable in Europe.

5. **“What is meant by the Bretton Woods Agreement?”**

   **Answer:** It was the post-war economic arrangement agreed at Bretton Woods in 1944. It created the IMF and World Bank, used fixed exchange rates, and aimed to support economic stability, reconstruction and full employment.

### Discuss

6. **“Imagine that you are an indentured Indian labourer in the Caribbean. Drawing from the details in this chapter, write a letter to your family describing your life and feelings.”**

   **Answer frame:** Write in the first person. Mention an agent's promises, the long voyage, hard plantation work, few legal rights, wage deductions or punishment, homesickness, and the new bonds, festivals or music created with other workers.

7. **“Explain the three types of movements or flows within international economic exchange. Find one example of each type of flow which involved India and Indians, and write a short account of it.”**

   **Answer:** Goods: Indian raw cotton, indigo, food grains and opium entered world trade. Labour: Indian indentured workers migrated to plantations in the Caribbean, Mauritius and Fiji. Capital: Indian bankers such as the Shikaripuri shroffs and Nattukottai Chettiars financed export agriculture in Asia.

8. **“Explain the causes of the Great Depression.”**

   **Answer:** Agricultural overproduction pushed prices down, so farmers produced even more and worsened the glut. Many countries depended on easily available US loans. When US lenders withdrew credit, banks and businesses failed, demand fell, unemployment rose and protectionist import duties reduced world trade further.

9. **“Explain what is referred to as the G-77 countries. In what ways can G-77 be seen as a reaction to the activities of the Bretton Woods twins?”**

   **Answer:** The G-77 was a group of developing countries that demanded a New International Economic Order. It reacted to IMF and World Bank arrangements dominated by industrial powers by asking for control over natural resources, more development aid, fairer raw-material prices and better market access.

### Project

**“Find out more about gold and diamond mining in South Africa in the nineteenth century. Who controlled the gold and diamond companies? Who were the miners and what were their lives like?”**

Use dated, reliable sources. Organise the project under ownership, workforce, recruitment, wages, living compounds, safety and racial control; do not turn it into a prediction-based exam answer.

## Common mistakes students make

- Saying globalisation began only in the twentieth century.
- Writing only “trade” and forgetting the linked flows of labour and capital.
- Calling indenture fully free migration; contracts, deception and coercion mattered.
- Treating technology as the only cause of change and ignoring colonial power and investment.
- Confusing the First World War with the Great Depression.
- Saying Bretton Woods created only the World Bank; it created both the IMF and World Bank framework.

## Board-answer tips

- For a cause-and-effect answer, use arrows or clear linking words: “because”, “therefore”, “as a result”.
- For the three flows, name **goods, labour and capital** first, then give one India-linked example of each.
- In a Great Depression answer, cover causes, world effects and India's different rural and urban experiences.
- Use the textbook's repeated 2.4 numbering if referring to sections; do not silently renumber the indenture section.
- “Technology helped” is incomplete unless the answer also states who financed, controlled and benefited from it.

### Answer check

1. Regular ocean routes shortened effective distance and rapidly moved goods, people, ideas and disease.
2. Goods, labour and capital.
3. Frozen meat could travel cheaply over long distances, lowering prices and widening access.
4. Massive cattle deaths destroyed livelihoods; colonial powers controlled cattle and pushed Africans into wage work.
5. Workers faced deceptive recruitment, harsh conditions, few rights and punishment despite a formal contract.
6. Britain's surplus with India paid its deficits with other countries and helped meet home charges.
7. Wartime lending changed the US from international debtor to creditor.
8. Any two: resource control, more development help, fair raw-material prices, better access for manufactured goods.

## One-minute revision

- Pre-modern routes moved goods, beliefs and germs.
- The nineteenth-century economy joined goods, labour and capital.
- Colonialism made many global links unequal.
- Refrigeration, railways and steamships changed food trade.
- Indentured workers faced coercion but created new cultures.
- The First World War shifted financial power towards the US.
- Overproduction and withdrawn US loans helped cause the Great Depression.
- Bretton Woods created the IMF-World Bank system; the G-77 later demanded a fairer order.

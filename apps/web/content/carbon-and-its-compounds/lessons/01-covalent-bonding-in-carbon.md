# 4.1 Bonding in carbon - the covalent bond

Carbon forms most of its compounds by sharing electrons. This type of bond is called a covalent bond.

Do not read this as "carbon simply joins with something". The important idea is: every atom is trying to become stable by filling its outermost shell. Carbon has four electrons in its outermost shell, so it needs a clever way to complete that shell without creating a very unstable ion.

## Why carbon shares electrons

Carbon has atomic number `6`.

Its electronic configuration is:

`2, 4`

Carbon has four valence electrons. To complete its outer shell, carbon would need four more electrons.

But:
- losing four electrons would need a very large amount of energy
- gaining four electrons would make a `C4-` ion with ten electrons held by only six protons

So carbon shares electrons with other atoms.

Think of carbon as having four "half-filled seats" in its outer shell. It does not throw away all four seats, and it does not grab four full seats from somewhere else. Instead, it shares one seat at a time with nearby atoms. After sharing, carbon can count the shared electrons as part of its own outer shell, and the other atom can also count the same shared pair.

That is why the textbook says the shared electrons belong to the outermost shells of both atoms.

## Step-by-step idea of a shared pair

Take hydrogen first, because it is the simplest.

Hydrogen has one electron in the K shell. The K shell is complete with two electrons. So one hydrogen atom is short of one electron.

If two hydrogen atoms come close:

1. hydrogen A brings one electron
2. hydrogen B brings one electron
3. the two electrons are shared between both atoms
4. hydrogen A now "sees" two electrons near it
5. hydrogen B also "sees" two electrons near it

The two shared electrons form one shared pair. One shared pair is one single covalent bond.

Line form:

`H-H`

The line does not mean a stick is physically present. It is a shortcut for one shared pair of electrons.

## Covalent bond

A covalent bond is formed when two atoms share one or more pairs of electrons.

In a shared pair:
- each atom contributes one electron
- both atoms count the shared pair as part of their outer shell

Memory line:
`Covalent = co-sharing of valence electrons.`

Important:
- one shared pair = single bond
- two shared pairs = double bond
- three shared pairs = triple bond

Before drawing any structure, ask: how many electrons does each atom need to complete its outer shell?

## Simple electron-sharing examples

[[figure:figure-4-1-to-4-5-covalent-bonding]]

### Hydrogen molecule

Each hydrogen atom has one electron. Two hydrogen atoms share one pair of electrons.

`H-H`

Each H gets the stable helium-like K shell arrangement of two electrons.

### Chlorine molecule

Each chlorine atom needs one more electron to complete its octet. Two chlorine atoms share one pair.

`Cl-Cl`

Chlorine has electronic configuration `2, 8, 7`. It is short of one electron, so two chlorine atoms solve the problem by sharing one pair.

### Oxygen molecule

Each oxygen atom needs two more electrons, so two oxygen atoms share two pairs.

`O=O`

Oxygen has electronic configuration `2, 6`. One shared pair would still leave each oxygen short by one electron. So the two oxygen atoms share two pairs. Two shared pairs are shown by a double line.

### Nitrogen molecule

Each nitrogen atom needs three more electrons, so two nitrogen atoms share three pairs.

`N#N`

Nitrogen has electronic configuration `2, 5`. It needs three more electrons to complete its octet. So two nitrogen atoms share three pairs. That is why nitrogen has a triple bond.

## How methane forms, slowly

Methane is the first carbon compound where this idea becomes very important.

Formula: `CH4`

Start with the needs:
- carbon has four valence electrons and needs four more
- each hydrogen has one electron and needs one more

Now pair them patiently:

1. Carbon shares one electron with the first hydrogen. This makes one `C-H` bond.
2. Carbon shares one electron with the second hydrogen. Now there are two `C-H` bonds.
3. Carbon shares one electron with the third hydrogen. Now there are three `C-H` bonds.
4. Carbon shares one electron with the fourth hydrogen. Now there are four `C-H` bonds.

After this:
- carbon is surrounded by four shared pairs, so it completes its octet
- each hydrogen is part of one shared pair, so each hydrogen completes its duet

Line structure:

```text
   H
   |
H--C--H
   |
   H
```

Do not say "carbon gives one electron to hydrogen". In covalent bonding, carbon and hydrogen share the pair. Both atoms benefit from the same pair.

## How carbon dioxide forms

Carbon dioxide is `CO2`.

Start with the needs:
- carbon needs four more electrons
- each oxygen needs two more electrons

There are two oxygen atoms. Carbon can share two pairs with the oxygen on the left and two pairs with the oxygen on the right.

That gives:

`O=C=O`

Meaning:
- left `C=O` is one double bond
- right `C=O` is one double bond
- total shared pairs around carbon = four

So carbon completes its octet, and each oxygen also completes its octet.

## Methane and carbon dioxide quick comparison

### Methane

Methane is `CH4`.

Carbon shares one electron with each of four hydrogen atoms. This gives carbon four single covalent bonds.

### Carbon dioxide

Carbon dioxide is `CO2`.

Carbon forms two double bonds, one with each oxygen atom:

`O=C=O`

Electron-dot idea:
Each oxygen shares two pairs of electrons with carbon, so carbon completes its octet.

Memory trick:
`CH4`: four hydrogens, four single bonds.
`CO2`: two oxygens, two double bonds.

### Sulphur molecule clue

The textbook asks about sulphur, `S8`.

At Class 10 level, remember:
- sulphur has six valence electrons
- each sulphur atom forms two single covalent bonds
- eight sulphur atoms join in a ring

## Properties of covalent compounds

Most covalent compounds:
- have low melting and boiling points
- are poor conductors of electricity

Reason:
Inside each molecule, the covalent bonds are strong. But the force of attraction between one molecule and another molecule is usually weak. So less heat is needed to separate the molecules, giving low melting and boiling points.

They are also made of neutral molecules, not free ions. Since there are no free charged particles moving through the substance, electricity does not pass easily.

Textbook examples of low melting and boiling points include methane, ethanol, chloroform, and acetic acid.

### Table 4.1 - source values

| Compound | Melting point (K) | Boiling point (K) |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Acetic acid (`CH3COOH`) | 290 | 391 |
| Chloroform (`CHCl3`) | 209 | 334 |
| Ethanol (`CH3CH2OH`) | 156 | 351 |
| Methane (`CH4`) | 90 | 111 |

These low values, compared with ionic compounds, support the conclusion that
the intermolecular attractions in most covalent compounds are weak.

## Carbon allotropes - NCERT knowledge box

[[figure:figure-4-allotropes]]

- In **diamond**, each carbon bonds to four other carbon atoms in a rigid
  three-dimensional structure. It is extremely hard.
- In **graphite**, each carbon bonds to three other carbon atoms in layered
  hexagonal sheets. The layers slide, so graphite is smooth and slippery; its
  mobile electrons allow it to conduct electricity.
- **C60 fullerene** has carbon atoms arranged like a football. It was named
  after Buckminster Fuller.

## Questions and answers

### 4.1 Bonding in carbon - the covalent bond

Question:
Why does carbon form covalent bonds?

Answer:
Carbon has four valence electrons, and gaining or losing four electrons is difficult, so it shares electrons instead.

Question:
How does methane form?

Answer:
Carbon shares one electron with each of four hydrogen atoms, so methane has four covalent bonds.

Question:
Why are covalent compounds generally poor conductors?

Answer:
They usually do not have free ions or free electrons to carry charge.

Do You Know:
- Covalent bonding is common in organic compounds because carbon can share electrons in many stable ways.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: Carbon ions

Question:
Why does carbon not simply lose four electrons?

Answer:
Removing four electrons needs a very large amount of energy, so carbon shares electrons instead.

### Example 2: Covalent conductivity

Question:
Why do most covalent compounds not conduct electricity?

Answer:
They do not have free ions or free electrons to carry charge.

### Example 3: Electron-dot question

Question:
What would be the electron-dot structure of carbon dioxide?

Answer:
Carbon is in the centre. It shares two pairs of electrons with each oxygen atom. In line form, write `O=C=O`.

### Example 4: Methane bond formation

Question:
Explain the covalent bond formation in methane.

Answer:
Carbon has four valence electrons and each hydrogen has one valence electron. Carbon shares one electron with each of four hydrogen atoms. Four shared pairs are formed, giving four single `C-H` covalent bonds. Carbon completes its octet and each hydrogen completes its duet.

### Example 5: `CH3Cl` from the exercise

Question:
Explain the nature of the covalent bond using bond formation in `CH3Cl`.

Answer:
Carbon is the central atom. It shares one pair of electrons with each of three hydrogen atoms and one pair with one chlorine atom. Thus carbon forms four single covalent bonds. The chlorine atom has seven valence electrons and needs one more electron, so sharing one pair with carbon completes chlorine's octet.

## Common mistakes

1. Saying carbon mainly forms ionic compounds.
2. Forgetting that a double bond means two shared pairs.
3. Drawing methane with only one or two bonds around carbon.
4. Saying every covalent compound has no exception. Graphite conducts electricity because of its structure.

## One-minute revision

Carbon has four valence electrons. Gaining or losing four electrons is difficult, so carbon shares electrons and forms covalent bonds. One shared pair is one single bond, two shared pairs make a double bond, and three shared pairs make a triple bond. Methane has four single bonds. Carbon dioxide has two double bonds. Most covalent compounds have low melting and boiling points and are poor conductors because they do not form free ions.

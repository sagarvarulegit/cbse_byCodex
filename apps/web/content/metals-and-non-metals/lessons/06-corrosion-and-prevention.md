# 3.4 Occurrence of Metals

The earth’s crust is the main source of metals. Seawater also contains soluble
salts such as sodium chloride and magnesium chloride.

## Mineral, ore and gangue

- **Minerals:** elements or compounds that occur naturally in the earth’s
  crust.
- **Ores:** minerals containing enough of a particular metal for the metal to
  be extracted profitably.
- **Gangue:** unwanted impurities such as soil and sand mixed with an ore.

Every ore is a mineral, but every mineral is not an ore. Profitability and
metal content are the deciding ideas.

# 3.4.1 Extraction of Metals

The form in which a metal occurs and the extraction method both depend on its
position in the reactivity series.

## Figure 3.9 — activity-series bands

[[demo:figure-3-9-reactivity-and-metallurgy]]

| Reactivity band | Metals | Usual occurrence | Main extraction method |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| High | K, Na, Ca, Mg, Al | Never free; stable compounds | Electrolytic reduction |
| Middle | Zn, Fe, Pb and similar metals | Mainly sulphides, carbonates or oxides | Convert to oxide, then reduce with carbon or a more reactive metal |
| Low | Cu, Hg, Ag, Au and similar metals | Some native; some as sulphides/oxides | Heating/roasting; oxide may reduce on heating |

Gold, silver, platinum and copper may occur in the free state. Copper and
silver also occur in combined form. Oxygen is abundant and reactive, so many
ores are oxides.

## Figure 3.10 — complete metallurgy flow

[[demo:figure-3-10-extraction-flow]]

`Ore → Enrichment → Conversion to oxide when needed → Reduction to crude metal → Refining → Pure metal`

- Low activity: heat sulphide/oxide; reduction may occur by heating.
- Middle activity: roast/calcine, then reduce the oxide.
- High activity: electrolyse the molten compound.

**Metallurgy** is the extraction of metals from ores and their refining for
use.

# 3.4.2 Enrichment of Ores

Ore mined from the earth contains gangue. Before extraction, the gangue is
removed by a process based on differences in physical or chemical properties
of the ore and gangue. Different ores therefore need different separation
techniques.

# 3.4.3 Extracting Metals Low in the Activity Series

Low-reactivity metal oxides may be reduced by heating alone.

## Mercury from cinnabar

Cinnabar is HgS. It is first heated in air to form mercuric oxide, then HgO
decomposes on further heating:

`2HgS(s) + 3O₂(g) →[heat] 2HgO(s) + 2SO₂(g)`

`2HgO(s) →[heat] 2Hg(l) + O₂(g)`

## Copper from copper sulphide

`2Cu₂S(s) + 3O₂(g) →[heat] 2Cu₂O(s) + 2SO₂(g)`

The remaining Cu₂S reduces Cu₂O:

`2Cu₂O(s) + Cu₂S(s) →[heat] 6Cu(s) + SO₂(g)`

# 3.4.4 Extracting Metals in the Middle of the Activity Series

Moderately reactive metals such as zinc, iron, lead and copper usually occur
as sulphides or carbonates. It is easier to reduce an oxide than a sulphide or
carbonate, so the ore is first converted to an oxide.

## Roasting versus calcination

| Process | Ore type | Air supply | Zinc example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roasting | Sulphide ore | Strong heating in excess air | `2ZnS + 3O₂ → 2ZnO + 2SO₂` |
| Calcination | Carbonate ore | Strong heating in limited air | `ZnCO₃ → ZnO + CO₂` |

## Reduction with carbon

`ZnO(s) + C(s) → Zn(s) + CO(g)`

Obtaining a metal from its oxide is a **reduction** process.

## Displacement using a more reactive metal

Highly reactive metals such as sodium, calcium and aluminium can reduce the
oxides of less reactive metals:

`3MnO₂(s) + 4Al(s) → 3Mn(l) + 2Al₂O₃(s) + heat`

- Aluminium is oxidised to Al₂O₃.
- MnO₂ is reduced to Mn.

## Figure 3.11 — thermit reaction

[[demo:figure-3-11-thermit-welding]]

`Fe₂O₃(s) + 2Al(s) → 2Fe(l) + Al₂O₃(s) + heat`

The reaction is highly exothermic and produces molten iron. It is used to join
railway tracks and repair cracked machine parts.

# 3.4.5 Extracting Metals towards the Top of the Activity Series

Carbon cannot reduce compounds of sodium, magnesium, calcium or aluminium
because these metals have a greater affinity for oxygen than carbon. They are
obtained by **electrolytic reduction**.

For electrolysis of molten sodium chloride:

- cathode is negative; metal deposits:
  `Na⁺ + e⁻ → Na`
- anode is positive; chlorine forms:
  `2Cl⁻ → Cl₂ + 2e⁻`

Sodium, magnesium and calcium are obtained from molten chlorides. Aluminium is
obtained by electrolytic reduction of aluminium oxide.

Exam rule: in extraction electrolysis, **reduction occurs at the cathode** and
**oxidation occurs at the anode**.

# 3.4.6 Refining of Metals

Reduction produces an impure metal. Electrolytic refining is widely used for
copper, zinc, tin, nickel, silver and gold.

## Figure 3.12 — electrolytic refining of copper

[[demo:figure-3-12-copper-refining]]

| Part | What is used |
| --- | --- |
| Anode (+) | Thick slab of impure copper |
| Cathode (−) | Thin strip of pure copper |
| Electrolyte | Acidified copper sulphate solution |

On passing current:

- copper from the anode dissolves into the electrolyte;
- an equivalent amount of pure copper deposits on the cathode;
- soluble impurities enter the solution;
- insoluble impurities settle below the anode as **anode mud**.

Useful electrode equations:

`Anode: Cu(s) → Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻`

`Cathode: Cu²⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ → Cu(s)`

# NCERT Questions — after 3.4

## 1. Define mineral, ore and gangue.

### Answer

A mineral is a naturally occurring element or compound in the earth’s crust.
An ore is a mineral from which a metal can be extracted profitably. Gangue is
the unwanted soil, sand or other impurity mixed with an ore.

## 2. Name two metals found in nature in the free state.

### Answer

Gold and platinum. Silver and copper are also found in the free state.

## 3. What chemical process is used for obtaining a metal from its oxide?

### Answer

**Reduction.** Depending on reactivity, the oxide is reduced by heating,
carbon, a more reactive metal, or electrolytic reduction.

# 3.5 Corrosion

Corrosion is the gradual deterioration of a metal due to reaction with its
surroundings.

## Textbook examples

- Silver turns black because silver sulphide forms by reaction with sulphur
  compounds in air.
- Copper develops a green coating of **basic copper carbonate** by reaction
  with moist carbon dioxide.
- Iron develops a brown, flaky coating called rust after long exposure to
  moist air.

## Activity 3.14 / Figure 3.13 — conditions for rusting

[[demo:activity-3-14-rusting-conditions]]

Put clean iron nails in three corked tubes:

| Tube | Setup | Air available? | Water available? | Result |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | Ordinary water with air space | Yes | Yes | Nails rust |
| B | Boiled distilled water covered with about 1 mL oil | No dissolved air | Yes | No rust |
| C | Anhydrous calcium chloride in dry air | Yes | No; CaCl₂ absorbs moisture | No rust |

**Conclusion:** iron rusts only when both air (oxygen) and water are present.

# 3.5.1 Prevention of Corrosion

Rusting of iron can be prevented by:

- painting;
- oiling or greasing;
- galvanising;
- chrome plating;
- anodising;
- alloying.

## Galvanisation

Galvanisation coats iron or steel with a thin layer of zinc. Zinc blocks air
and water and, because it is more reactive than iron, continues to protect the
iron even if the coating is locally damaged.

## Alloying

An **alloy** is a homogeneous mixture of two or more metals, or a metal and a
non-metal. It is prepared by melting the primary metal, dissolving the other
elements in definite proportions and cooling the mixture.

- Pure iron is soft and stretches easily when hot.
- About 0.05% carbon makes iron hard and strong.
- Iron + nickel + chromium makes hard, rust-resistant stainless steel.
- An alloy containing mercury is an **amalgam**.
- Brass = copper + zinc.
- Bronze = copper + tin.
- Solder = lead + tin; its low melting point suits joining electrical wires.
- Alloys generally have lower electrical conductivity and melting point than
  the pure metals used to make them.

## Do You Know — gold purity

Pure **24-carat gold** is too soft for jewellery. In India, **22-carat gold**
is commonly used: 22 parts pure gold are alloyed with 2 parts copper or silver.

## More to Know — Delhi iron pillar

The iron pillar near Qutub Minar was made more than 1600 years ago by Indian
iron workers using a process that gave high rust resistance. It is about 8 m
high and weighs 6 tonnes (6000 kg).

# NCERT Questions — after 3.5

## 1. Metallic oxides of zinc, magnesium and copper are heated with zinc, magnesium and copper. Where does displacement occur?

### Answer

A metal displaces another metal from its oxide only when it is more reactive.

| Oxide heated with → | Zinc | Magnesium | Copper |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Zinc oxide | No | **Yes** | No |
| Magnesium oxide | No | No | No |
| Copper oxide | **Yes** | **Yes** | No |

Examples:

`Mg + ZnO → MgO + Zn`

`Zn + CuO → ZnO + Cu`

`Mg + CuO → MgO + Cu`

## 2. Which metals do not corrode easily?

### Answer

Low-reactivity noble metals such as gold and platinum do not corrode easily.

## 3. What are alloys?

### Answer

Alloys are homogeneous mixtures of two or more metals, or of a metal and a
non-metal, made to obtain desired properties.

## Extraction decision ladder

1. Locate the metal in the activity series.
2. High → electrolysis of a molten compound.
3. Middle → convert sulphide/carbonate to oxide, then reduce.
4. Low → heat/roast; oxide may reduce on heating.
5. Refine the crude metal, commonly by electrolysis.

## Common mistakes

- Defining every mineral as an ore.
- Swapping roasting and calcination.
- Saying carbon reduces sodium oxide.
- Making impure copper the cathode during refining.
- Calling anode mud soluble impurities.
- Saying rusting needs “air or water”; it needs **air and water**.
- Calling 22-carat gold pure gold.

## One-minute revision

Reactivity decides occurrence and extraction. Low-activity metals need mild
reduction, middle metals are converted to oxides and reduced, and high metals
need electrolysis. Refining gives pure metal. Corrosion is limited by coatings
or alloying; rusting specifically needs both air and water.

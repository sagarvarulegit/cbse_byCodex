# Chapter 2: Federalism

## Hook

Who should decide what your school teaches: only the national government, only the State Government, or both according to agreed rules? Federalism answers this kind of question by dividing power among governments at different levels.

**Chapter goal:** Understand the meaning and main features of federalism, explain how India’s Constitution divides power, and see how language policy, Centre–State relations and decentralisation have made Indian federalism work in practice.

## Overview

The previous chapter introduced the vertical division of power. This chapter calls that arrangement **federalism**. It first explains the idea, then studies the constitutional design and political practice of federalism in India, and finally examines local government as its third tier.

## What is federalism?

Federalism is a system of government in which power is divided between a central authority and constituent units such as States or provinces. Usually, one government handles matters of common national interest and regional governments handle much of their State’s day-to-day administration. Each level enjoys power independently of the other.

In a **unitary system**, there may be only one level of government, or the sub-units remain subordinate to the central government. The centre can order them. In a federation, a State Government has powers of its own and is not answerable to the central government for those subjects.

### Seven key features

1. There are two or more levels or tiers of government.
2. Different tiers govern the same citizens, but each has its own jurisdiction in legislation, taxation and administration.
3. The Constitution specifies and guarantees each tier’s jurisdiction.
4. One level cannot unilaterally change the fundamental power-sharing provisions; both levels must consent.
5. Courts interpret the Constitution, and the highest court settles disputes between levels.
6. Each level has clearly specified revenue sources for financial autonomy.
7. The system protects national unity while accommodating regional diversity.

Federalism needs **mutual trust** and an **agreement to live together**.

### Two routes to federation

- **Coming together federation:** Independent States join to form a larger unit, pool sovereignty and keep their identity. Constituent States usually have equal power and are strong compared with the federal government. Examples: the USA, Switzerland and Australia.
- **Holding together federation:** A large country divides power between the national and constituent governments. The centre is usually stronger, and constituent units may have unequal powers. Examples: India, Spain and Belgium.

## What makes India a federal country?

India became independent after a painful Partition and the integration of princely states. The Constitution declared India a **Union of States**. It did not use the word “federation,” but its design follows federal principles.

The Constitution first provided two tiers—the Union Government and State Governments. A third tier of Panchayats and Municipalities was added later.

### Three-fold distribution of legislative power

| List | Who makes laws? | Textbook examples |
|---|---|---|
| Union List | Union Government | defence, foreign affairs, banking, communications, currency |
| State List | State Governments | police, trade, commerce, agriculture, irrigation |
| Concurrent List | Union and State Governments | education, forests, trade unions, marriage, adoption, succession |

If Union and State laws conflict on a Concurrent List subject, the Union law prevails. The Union Government also legislates on **residuary subjects**, including subjects such as computer software that were not imagined when the Constitution was written.

India is a holding together federation, so all units do not have identical powers:

- Some States, including Assam, Nagaland, Arunachal Pradesh and Mizoram, receive special powers under Article 371 because of their social and historical circumstances. These protect matters such as indigenous land rights, culture and preferential government employment.
- Union Territories such as Chandigarh, Lakshadweep and Delhi are too small to be independent States or could not be merged with existing States. The Central Government has special powers in these areas.

Parliament cannot change the Union–State power arrangement by itself. A change must pass both Houses with at least a two-thirds majority and then be ratified by the legislatures of at least half of all States. High Courts and the Supreme Court settle power disputes. Both Union and State Governments raise revenue through taxes for their assigned responsibilities.

## How is federalism practised?

Constitutional rules are necessary but not enough. Indian federalism succeeded because democratic politics developed respect for diversity, the spirit of power-sharing and a desire to live together. Its practice became stronger through linguistic States, a flexible language policy and changed Centre–State relations.

## Linguistic States

Creating States mainly on the basis of language was the first major test of democratic politics after Independence. Leaders first feared that linguistic States would divide India. Experience showed the opposite: they made the country more united and administration easier.

Some States were formed not by language but to recognise cultural, ethnic or geographical differences. The textbook names Nagaland, Uttarakhand and Jharkhand as examples.

## Language policy

The Constitution did not give any one language the status of national language. Hindi was identified as an **official language**, but it was the mother tongue of only about 40 per cent of Indians in the textbook’s discussion. Safeguards protected other languages:

- Hindi and 21 other languages were recognised as Scheduled Languages in the constitutional account given in the main text; the later 2011 language box lists all **22 Scheduled Languages**.
- A candidate for a Central Government examination may choose any Scheduled Language.
- States have their own official languages, and much State work is done in the concerned State’s official language.
- The Constitution expected official use of English to end in 1965. Non-Hindi-speaking States opposed this, so English continued alongside Hindi.
- The Union promotes Hindi but cannot impose it on States that speak other languages.

This flexibility helped India avoid the kind of language conflict seen in Sri Lanka.

## Centre-State relations

For a long time, the same party ruled at the Centre and in most States. State Governments often did not act as autonomous federal units. When rival parties ruled a State, the Central Government sometimes misused constitutional power to dismiss that government.

After **1990**, regional parties grew and coalition governments began at the Centre. National parties needed alliances with regional parties. This created a culture of power-sharing and greater respect for State autonomy. A major Supreme Court judgment also made arbitrary dismissal of State Governments more difficult. Federal power-sharing therefore became more effective than in the early years.

**Key term — Coalition government:** A government formed by at least two political parties, usually through an alliance and a common programme.

### Textbook source guide

- **Glossary box — “Coalition government”:** It defines a government formed by the coming together of at least two political parties that usually adopt a common programme.

## Decentralisation in India

India is too large and diverse to be governed only by Union and State tiers. **Decentralisation** means taking power from Central and State Governments and giving it to local government. Local people know local problems and priorities better and can participate directly.

Panchayats and municipalities existed earlier, but they were controlled by State Governments, lacked their own powers and resources, and did not hold regular elections. The **1992 constitutional amendment** strengthened the third tier:

- regular local elections became constitutionally mandatory;
- seats and executive-head posts were reserved for Scheduled Castes, Scheduled Tribes and Other Backward Classes;
- at least one-third of all positions were reserved for women;
- each State received an independent State Election Commission for Panchayat and municipal elections; and
- State Governments had to share some powers and revenue with local bodies, although the amount varies by State.

### Rural local government

- A village or group of villages has a **Gram Panchayat**, made up of elected ward members called panch and a president or sarpanch.
- All village voters form the **Gram Sabha**. It meets at least two or three times a year to approve the Gram Panchayat’s annual budget and review its work.
- Several Gram Panchayats form a **Panchayat Samiti**, block or mandal.
- The Panchayat Samitis or mandals of a district form a **Zilla Parishad**. Its elected chairperson is the political head.

### Urban local government

Municipalities govern towns, and Municipal Corporations govern big cities. Elected representatives control them. A municipal chairperson heads a municipality; the corresponding officer in a Municipal Corporation is called the mayor.

India now has about **36 lakh** elected representatives in Panchayats, Municipalities and related bodies. Constitutional status deepened democracy and women’s representation. Yet Gram Sabhas are not always regular, and many States have not transferred enough power or resources, so full self-government remains unfinished.

### 2. Identify and shade three federal countries (other than India) on a blank outline political map of the world.

**Answer:** Any three federal countries shown in the chapter map may be used, such as the **USA, Canada and Australia**.

### 3. Point out one feature in the practice of federalism in India that is similar to and one feature that is different from that of Belgium.

**Answer:** Similarity: both constitutionally divide power between central and regional governments. Difference: Belgium also has community governments for language communities and gives equal central representation to Dutch- and French-speaking groups, while India’s holding together federation generally gives the Union Government greater power than the States.

### 4. What is the main difference between a federal form of government and a unitary one? Explain with an example.

**Answer:** In a federal system, the Constitution divides power between levels and each has an independent jurisdiction; the centre cannot order a State on a State subject. India is federal. In a unitary system, sub-units are subordinate and the centre may withdraw or direct their powers; Sri Lanka is the chapter’s example.

### 5. State any two differences between the local government before and after the Constitutional amendment in 1992.

**Answer:** Before 1992, local elections were irregular and local bodies lacked independent constitutional power and resources. After the amendment, regular elections became mandatory, reservations were provided, State Election Commissions were created, and States had to share powers and revenue with local bodies.

## Common mistakes

- Calling Hindi India’s national language. The Constitution identifies Hindi as an official language and recognises linguistic diversity.
- Saying every federation gives equal powers to all States. Holding together federations may have unequal units and a stronger centre.
- Mixing horizontal separation of organs with federal division among levels.
- Putting education only in the State List. It is in the Concurrent List in the textbook.
- Treating Gram Panchayat and Gram Sabha as the same: the Panchayat is elected; the Gram Sabha includes all village voters.

## Board tips

- For a federal–unitary comparison, write about constitutional status, independence of tiers and the centre’s power to issue orders.
- In List questions, first decide whether the subject needs one national policy, local control, or shared action.
- Explain Indian federalism as **constitutional design + democratic practice**; do not stop after naming the three lists.
- Use the 1992 amendment points when asked how decentralisation became effective.

## One-minute revision

Federalism divides power constitutionally among government levels. Coming together federations usually have strong, equal States; holding together federations usually have a stronger centre. India uses Union, State and Concurrent Lists, with residuary power at the Union. Linguistic States, flexible language policy, coalitions and judicial protection strengthened federal practice. The 1992 amendment made elected rural and urban local bodies the third tier.

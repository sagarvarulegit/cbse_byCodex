## 2.1-2.2 Understanding acids, bases, and neutralisation

Acids usually taste sour and turn blue litmus red.

Bases usually taste bitter, feel soapy, and turn red litmus blue.

Common indicators:
- litmus
- methyl orange
- phenolphthalein
- turmeric

## 2.1 continued - Reactions of acids and bases

### acids with metals

Acid + metal -> salt + hydrogen gas

Example:
`Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2`

Hydrogen gas burns with a pop sound.

### acids with metal carbonates

Acid + metal carbonate -> salt + carbon dioxide + water

Example:
`Na2CO3 + 2HCl -> 2NaCl + CO2 + H2O`

Carbon dioxide turns lime water milky.

### acids with metal oxides

Acid + metal oxide -> salt + water

Metal oxides are basic in nature.

### bases with non-metal oxides

Base + non-metal oxide -> salt + water

Non-metal oxides are acidic in nature.

Example:
`Ca(OH)2 + CO2 -> CaCO3 + H2O`

## 2.3-2.4 pH scale and importance in daily life

Acids produce `H+` ions in water.

Bases produce `OH-` ions in water.

Dry acids often do not show acidic behaviour because ion formation needs water.

pH tells how acidic or basic a solution is.

- pH less than 7: acidic
- pH equal to 7: neutral
- pH greater than 7: basic

Strong acid means very low pH. Strong base means very high pH.

Everyday pH examples:
- stomach uses hydrochloric acid
- antacids neutralise excess stomach acid
- tooth decay starts when mouth pH falls below about 5.5
- plants need suitable soil pH

## 2.5 More about salts and important compounds

### family of salts

Salts having the same positive or negative ion belong to the same family.

Example:
`NaCl`, `Na2SO4`, and `NaNO3` are sodium salts.

### common salt

Common salt is sodium chloride, `NaCl`.

It is a raw material for:
- sodium hydroxide
- bleaching powder
- baking soda
- washing soda

### bleaching powder

Formula commonly written as `CaOCl2`.

Uses:
- bleaching cotton and linen
- disinfecting drinking water

### baking soda

Sodium hydrogencarbonate: `NaHCO3`.

Uses:
- baking powder
- antacid
- soda-acid fire extinguishers

### washing soda

Sodium carbonate decahydrate: `Na2CO3.10H2O`.

Uses:
- cleaning
- removing permanent hardness of water

### plaster of Paris

Calcium sulphate hemihydrate: `CaSO4.1/2H2O`.

It sets into gypsum when mixed with water.

## exam-friendly summary notes

- Acids give `H+` in water.
- Bases give `OH-` in water.
- Neutralisation forms salt and water.
- Metal carbonates with acids release `CO2`.
- pH is a measure of acidity or basicity.
- Common salt is the starting material for many useful chemicals.

## common mistakes students make

1. Saying acids produce `H+` without water.
2. Confusing baking soda and washing soda.
3. Forgetting that non-metal oxides are acidic.
4. Thinking neutral pH always means pure water only.
5. Writing plaster of Paris as gypsum.

## board tips

- Memorise key formulae: `NaCl`, `NaOH`, `NaHCO3`, `Na2CO3.10H2O`, `CaSO4.1/2H2O`.
- For pH questions, write the scale clearly.
- Mention the gas test for hydrogen and carbon dioxide.

## solved practice set

### question 1
What happens when zinc reacts with dilute hydrochloric acid?

Answer:
Zinc chloride and hydrogen gas are formed.
`Zn + 2HCl -> ZnCl2 + H2`

### question 2
Why does dry HCl gas not change dry litmus paper?

Answer:
Acidic behaviour needs `H+` ions, and ions form in water. Dry HCl does not ionise enough without water.

### question 3
Name the compound used for setting fractured bones.

Answer:
Plaster of Paris.

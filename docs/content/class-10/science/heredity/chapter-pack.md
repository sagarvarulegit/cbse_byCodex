# Heredity

## chapter goal

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:
- explain variation during reproduction
- understand inherited traits
- use Mendel's rules for simple inheritance
- explain how traits are expressed
- describe sex determination in humans

## 1) variation during reproduction

Variation means differences among individuals of the same species.

Asexual reproduction gives fewer variations because offspring come from one parent.

Sexual reproduction gives more variation because genes come from two parents.

Variation helps populations survive environmental changes.

## 2) heredity

Heredity is the transfer of traits from parents to offspring.

Traits are controlled by genes.

Genes are segments of DNA present on chromosomes.

Each child receives genetic material from both parents.

## 3) inherited traits

Inherited traits are passed from parents to offspring through genes.

Examples:
- height of pea plant
- seed shape
- eye colour

Acquired traits are developed during life and are usually not inherited.

Example:
A wrestler's strong muscles are acquired, not directly inherited by children.

## 4) Mendel's experiment

Mendel studied pea plants.

He crossed tall plants with short plants.

In the first generation, all plants were tall. This showed tallness was dominant.

When first-generation plants were crossed, tall and short plants appeared in about `3:1` ratio.

This showed:
- traits are inherited as separate units
- one trait can dominate another
- recessive traits can reappear

## 5) dominant and recessive traits

A dominant trait expresses itself even when only one copy is present.

A recessive trait expresses only when both copies are recessive.

Example:
If `T` means tall and `t` means short:
- `TT`: tall
- `Tt`: tall
- `tt`: short

## 6) how traits get expressed

Genes control proteins.

Proteins influence body features.

Example:
A gene may control an enzyme needed to make a hormone. If the enzyme works, the plant grows tall. If it does not, the plant may remain short.

## 7) sex determination

Humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes.

One pair is sex chromosomes.

Females have `XX`.

Males have `XY`.

Mother always contributes `X`.

Father contributes either `X` or `Y`.

If child gets `XX`, the child is female.

If child gets `XY`, the child is male.

## exam-friendly summary notes

- Heredity is transfer of traits through genes.
- Genes are located on chromosomes.
- Dominant trait appears in `TT` and `Tt`.
- Recessive trait appears only in `tt`.
- Mendel's monohybrid cross gives `3:1` phenotype ratio in F2.
- Father's sperm determines sex in humans.

## common mistakes students make

1. Confusing inherited traits with acquired traits.
2. Saying recessive traits disappear permanently.
3. Forgetting that both parents contribute genes.
4. Blaming mother for sex of child, which is scientifically wrong.
5. Mixing genotype with phenotype.

## board tips

- Draw a simple Punnett square for inheritance questions.
- Use capital letter for dominant trait and small letter for recessive trait.
- For sex determination, clearly write `XX` and `XY`.
- Mention that acquired traits are not inherited through DNA changes in reproductive cells.

## solved practice set

### question 1
Why did all F1 pea plants become tall when tall and short plants were crossed?

Answer:
Tallness was dominant over shortness, so the dominant trait expressed in F1.

### question 2
Can an acquired trait be inherited?

Answer:
Usually no. It does not change DNA in reproductive cells.

### question 3
Who determines the sex of a human child?

Answer:
The father, because sperm may carry either `X` or `Y` chromosome.

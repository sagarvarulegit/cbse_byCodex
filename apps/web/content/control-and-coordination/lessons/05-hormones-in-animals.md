# 6.3 Hormones in Animals

Animals also use chemical coordination. Hormones are chemical messengers released by endocrine glands into the blood and carried to target organs.

## 6.3 Hormones in Animals

Hormones travel through blood and act on specific target organs. They help coordinate body functions that need wider, slower, and longer-lasting control than nerve impulses provide.

Adrenaline is released in scary situations. It prepares the body for fight or flight by increasing heart rate and breathing rate and by reducing blood flow to the skin and digestive system.

Iodine is needed by the thyroid gland to make thyroxin. Thyroxin regulates metabolism for body growth. If iodine is lacking, goitre can develop and the neck may swell.

Growth hormone is secreted by the pituitary gland and helps normal growth and
development. Its deficiency in childhood can lead to dwarfism, while excessive
secretion in childhood can produce abnormal height or gigantism.

Testosterone in males and oestrogen in females are associated with puberty changes.

Insulin is produced by the pancreas and regulates blood sugar. If blood sugar rises, the pancreas releases more insulin. When sugar levels fall, insulin secretion reduces. This is a feedback mechanism.

### Nervous control and hormonal control

| Feature | Nervous mechanism | Hormonal mechanism |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Signal | Electrical impulse in a neuron; chemical transmission at a synapse | Chemical messenger or hormone |
| Route | Along connected nerve cells | Through blood to target organs |
| Speed | Very fast | Usually slower |
| Reach | Precise, connected cells such as a particular muscle | Can be widespread, but only cells with the correct receptors respond |
| Duration | Usually short-lived | Often longer-lasting and can act steadily |

Both mechanisms coordinate the body. Nervous control is best for rapid,
specific action; hormones are useful when many tissues need coordinated,
persistent change, as during growth or an emergency.

### Table 6.1: Some important hormones and their functions

- Growth hormone - Pituitary gland - Stimulates growth in all organs
- Thyroxin - Thyroid gland - Regulates metabolism for body growth
- Insulin - Pancreas - Regulates blood sugar level
- Testosterone - Testes - Develops male sex organs and related changes
- Oestrogen - Ovaries - Develops female sex organs and regulates menstrual cycle
- Adrenaline - Adrenal gland - Prepares the body for emergencies
- Releasing hormones - Hypothalamus - Stimulate the pituitary gland to release hormones

### High-frequency hormone disorders

| Change | Consequence |
| --- | --- |
| Too little growth hormone during childhood | Dwarfism |
| Too much growth hormone during childhood | Gigantism or abnormal excessive height |
| Too little iodine, therefore too little thyroxin | Goitre; swollen neck may be visible |
| Too little effective insulin | Blood sugar rises; diabetes may result |

### Do You Know?

The hypothalamus plays an important role in the release of many hormones. When growth hormone level is low, it releases growth hormone releasing factor, which stimulates the pituitary gland to release growth hormone.

## Questions and answers

Question:
How does chemical coordination take place in animals?

Answer:
Chemical coordination takes place through hormones. Endocrine glands release hormones into the blood, and the hormones act on specific target organs.

Question:
Why is the use of iodised salt advisable?

Answer:
Iodised salt supplies iodine, which the thyroid gland needs to make thyroxin. This helps keep metabolism and growth normal and helps prevent goitre.

Question:
How does our body respond when adrenaline is secreted into the blood?

Answer:
The heart beats faster, breathing rate increases, blood is diverted towards skeletal muscles, and the body becomes ready for emergency action.

Question:
Why are some patients of diabetes treated by giving injections of insulin?

Answer:
They do not make enough insulin or cannot use it properly, so injections help regulate blood sugar level.

Which of the following is a plant hormone?
(a) Insulin
(b) Thyroxin
(c) Oestrogen

Answer:
(d) Cytokinin.

### Exercise 2

Question:
The gap between two neurons is called a
(a) dendrite.
(b) synapse.
(c) axon.

(b) synapse.

### Exercise 3

Question:
The brain is responsible for
(a) thinking.
(b) regulating the heart beat.
(c) balancing the body.
(d) all of the above.

Answer:
(d) all of the above.

### Exercise 4

Question:
What is the function of receptors in our body? Think of situations where receptors do not work properly. What problems are likely to arise?

Answer:
Receptors detect stimuli and start the nerve message. If receptors do not work properly, a person may not feel pain, smell, taste, or sense other changes correctly, so injuries or dangers may not be noticed quickly.

### Exercise 5

Question:
Draw the structure of a neuron and explain its function.

Answer:
A neuron has dendrites, a cell body, and an axon. It receives information at the dendrites, carries the impulse through the cell body and axon, and passes the message on to the next cell.

### Exercise 6

Question:
How does phototropism occur in plants?

Answer:
Auxin collects on the shady side of the shoot and makes those cells grow longer. The shoot bends towards light.

### Exercise 7

Question:
Which signals will get disrupted in case of a spinal cord injury?

Answer:
Reflex signals and many messages between the brain and different parts of the body will be disrupted.

### Exercise 8

Question:
How does chemical coordination occur in plants?

Answer:
Plants use hormones that move from the place where they are made to the place where they act. These hormones control growth and responses such as bending towards light or water.

### Exercise 9

Question:
What is the need for a system of control and coordination in an organism?

Answer:
The organism needs a system to detect changes, respond correctly, and keep different body parts working together.

### Exercise 10

Question:
How are involuntary actions and reflex actions different from each other?

Answer:
Involuntary actions happen without conscious control and are usually managed by brain centres such as the medulla. Reflex actions are quick automatic responses that use a reflex arc, often through the spinal cord.

### Exercise 11

Question:
Compare and contrast nervous and hormonal mechanisms for control and coordination in animals.

Answer:
The nervous system uses electrical impulses and works quickly on specific connected cells. Hormonal control uses chemical messengers, travels through blood, acts more slowly, and can reach target organs all over the body.

### Exercise 12

Question:
What is the difference between the manner in which movement takes place in a sensitive plant and the movement in our legs?

Answer:
Sensitive plant movement happens by changes in water content and does not involve growth. Movement in our legs happens through nerve impulses and muscle contraction.

## Solved examples

### Example 1

Question:
Why is the blood sugar level kept under control?

Answer:
Too much or too little sugar can harm the body. Insulin and feedback keep the level in a safe range.

### Example 2

Question:
Why does adrenaline help in an emergency?

Answer:
It prepares the body for fight or flight by increasing heartbeat, breathing, and blood flow to muscles.

### Example 3

Question:
Why is thyroxin linked with iodine?

Answer:
The thyroid gland needs iodine to make thyroxin. Without iodine, the hormone level can fall and goitre may develop.

## Common mistakes

1. Saying hormones travel through nerves.
2. Forgetting that hormones act on target organs.
3. Mixing up thyroxin and thyroid gland.
4. Confusing insulin with digestive enzymes.
5. Forgetting the feedback mechanism in hormone control.

## Board tips

## One-minute revision

Hormones are chemical messengers. In animals, endocrine glands release hormones into blood and specific target organs respond. Adrenaline handles emergency situations, thyroxin needs iodine, growth hormone supports growth, and insulin keeps blood sugar under control by feedback.

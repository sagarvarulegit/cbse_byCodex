# 5.4.2 Transportation in plants

Plants need transport systems because leaves make food, roots absorb water and minerals, and the distances between these parts can be large.

Plant transport is slower than animal transport because plants have lower energy needs and many dead cells in tissues. Still, tall plants need an organised system.

## Learning goals

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- compare xylem and phloem
- explain root pressure and transpiration pull
- describe Activity 5.8
- explain translocation and the role of ATP
- identify NCERT Figure 5.12

## Plant transport overview

Plants have two main transport pathways:

Xylem:
- transports water and minerals
- moves mainly from roots upward
- uses vessels and tracheids forming continuous channels

Phloem:
- transports products of photosynthesis
- moves food from leaves to storage organs and growing parts
- movement can be upward or downward depending on need

Memory line:
`Xylem lifts water; phloem shifts food.`

## Transport of water

At the roots, cells actively take up ions from soil.

This creates a concentration difference between root and soil. Water moves into the root to balance this difference.

This steady entry of water creates root pressure, which can push water upward in xylem.

But root pressure alone is not enough for very tall plants.

## Transpiration pull

Leaves lose water vapour through stomata. This is transpiration.

When water evaporates from leaf cells, it creates a suction pull. This pull draws water upward through the continuous xylem column.

Transpiration helps:
- upward movement of water and minerals
- temperature regulation by cooling leaves

Board phrase:
`Transpiration pull is the main force for upward water movement in tall plants.`

## Transport of food

Food made in leaves must be moved to other parts of the plant.

This movement is called translocation.

Phloem transports:
- sucrose
- amino acids
- other substances from leaves to storage organs, roots, fruits, seeds, and growing regions

Unlike simple water movement, translocation uses energy from ATP.

In phloem:
1. food molecules are loaded into sieve tubes using ATP
2. water enters due to osmotic pressure
3. pressure moves material to tissues with less pressure
4. food is unloaded where it is needed or stored

## Xylem vs phloem

Xylem:
- water and minerals
- mainly upward
- driven by root pressure and transpiration pull
- mostly dead conducting tissue

Phloem:
- food and other soluble products
- both directions depending on need
- requires ATP for loading
- living conducting tissue

## Questions and answers

Question:
What are the components of the transport system in human beings? What are the functions of these components?

Answer:
The human transport system has the heart, blood, and blood vessels. The heart pumps blood, blood carries oxygen, carbon dioxide, food, and wastes, and blood vessels carry blood to and from different body parts.

Question:
Why is it necessary to separate oxygenated and deoxygenated blood in mammals and birds?

Answer:
Mammals and birds need a high and efficient oxygen supply because they maintain constant body temperature and have high energy needs. Separation prevents oxygenated and deoxygenated blood from mixing.

Question:
What are the components of the transport system in highly organised plants?

Answer:
The transport system in highly organised plants is vascular tissue. It consists mainly of xylem, which transports water and minerals, and phloem, which transports food and other soluble products.

Question:
How are water and minerals transported in plants?

Answer:
Root cells actively take up ions from soil, and water enters the root. Root pressure helps push water upward, while transpiration from leaves creates the main pull that draws water and minerals through xylem.

Question:
How is food transported in plants?

Answer:
Food is transported by phloem through translocation. Sucrose is loaded into sieve tubes using ATP, water enters by osmosis, pressure builds up, and the food solution moves to regions where it is needed or stored.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: Xylem

Question:
What is transported by xylem?

Answer:
Xylem transports water and minerals from roots to other parts of the plant.

### Example 2: Transpiration

Question:
How does transpiration help water movement?

Answer:
Loss of water vapour from leaves creates a suction pull that draws water upward through xylem.

### Example 3: Phloem

Question:
Why is translocation in phloem considered an active process?

Answer:
Food is loaded into phloem using energy from ATP, so it is an active process.

### Example 4: Direction

Question:
Why can phloem transport be bidirectional?

Answer:
Food moves from source regions, such as leaves, to storage or growing regions wherever food is needed, so direction can change.

## Common mistakes

1. Saying xylem carries food.
2. Saying phloem carries only upward.
3. Forgetting ATP in translocation.
4. Writing that root pressure alone lifts water in tall trees.
5. Treating transpiration only as water loss and missing its pull.

## One-minute revision

Xylem moves water and minerals from roots, helped by root pressure and mainly by transpiration pull. Phloem moves food made in leaves to other parts of the plant. Food transport is called translocation and uses ATP. Activity 5.8 shows that plants lose water vapour through transpiration, not just from soil evaporation.

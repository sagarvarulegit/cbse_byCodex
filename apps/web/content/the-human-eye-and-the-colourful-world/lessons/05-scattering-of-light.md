# 10.6 Scattering of Light

# 10.6.1 Tyndall Effect

The Tyndall effect is scattering of light by colloidal particles, making a beam
path visible. Examples:

- sunlight entering a smoke-filled room through a small opening;
- sunlight passing through mist below a forest canopy.

Very fine particles scatter shorter wavelengths mainly; larger particles can
scatter longer wavelengths, and sufficiently large particles may make scattered
light look white.

# 10.6.2 Why Is the Clear Sky Blue?

<ActivityIllustration demoId="figure-10-blue-sky-scattering" />

Air molecules and very fine particles are smaller than visible wavelengths.
They scatter shorter-wavelength blue light much more strongly than
longer-wavelength red light. Scattered blue light enters our eyes, so the sky
looks blue.

Violet is scattered strongly too, but our eyes are less sensitive to it and
some violet is absorbed in the upper atmosphere; NCERT's key conclusion is
stronger scattering of blue than red.

## Red sunrise and sunset

Near the horizon sunlight travels a much longer atmospheric path. Most blue
light is scattered away before the direct light reaches us. The remaining
longer-wavelength red/orange light makes the Sun and surrounding sky look red.

Danger signals use red because it is scattered least by fog and smoke and can
be seen from farther away.

## Astronaut's dark sky

Outside Earth's atmosphere there are essentially no air particles to scatter
sunlight into the observer's eyes. Therefore the sky appears dark, not blue.

## Do not mix

- dispersion: colour-dependent refraction by a prism;
- atmospheric refraction: bending through changing air layers;
- scattering: redirection by particles.

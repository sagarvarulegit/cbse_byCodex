# Chapter 5: Consumer Rights

## Hook

A product is defective, the shopkeeper refuses help, and the receipt is missing. What can one buyer do against a powerful seller? Consumer rights turn that weak position into a claim for information, safety, choice and justice.

## Chapter goal

This chapter explains why markets need rules, how the consumer movement grew, which rights consumers possess, how complaints reach Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, why information and quality marks matter, and why rights become effective only when consumers act together and responsibly.

## THE CONSUMER IN THE MARKETPLACE

People enter the market as producers and consumers. A consumer buys final goods or services for use. Individual consumers are usually scattered and buy small quantities, while a large producer or seller may have greater information, money and power.

Exploitation may include underweight goods, hidden charges, adulterated or defective products, sale above the Maximum Retail Price, false claims, unsafe services and forced purchase of an unwanted item. Rules are needed because asking each buyer to “be careful” cannot correct a large power gap.

## CONSUMER MOVEMENT

The movement arose because sellers used unfair practices and consumers lacked an effective legal system. In India, shortages, hoarding, black marketing and food adulteration helped organise the movement in the 1960s. In the 1970s, groups wrote articles, held exhibitions and monitored ration shops and crowded passenger transport.

Public pressure helped secure the Consumer Protection Act 1986, popularly called COPRA. The textbook notes that the Act was amended in 2019.

### Consumers International

This special box states that the United Nations adopted the UN Guidelines for Consumer Protection in 1985. It describes Consumers International as an umbrella body for more than 200 member organisations from over 100 countries. The box shows that consumer protection is also an international movement.

## CONSUMER RIGHTS

Consumer protection is not a favour from a seller. It includes recognised rights that let buyers demand safe products, useful information, real choice, representation, redressal and education.

### SAFETY IS EVERYONE’S RIGHT

Consumers have the right to be protected from goods and services hazardous to life and property. Producers must follow safety rules, and public agencies must check compliance. Pressure cookers, LPG cylinders, medicines, buildings and medical treatment all require special care.

### Reji’s Suffering

The case describes Reji Mathew, who suffered permanent harm after improper anaesthesia during a tonsil operation. After the State Commission dismissed the complaint, his father appealed to the National Commission, which held the hospital responsible and ordered compensation. The case connects medical negligence with the right to safety and the right to redressal.

### Information about goods and services

The right to information covers ingredients, price, batch number, manufacturing and expiry dates, the manufacturer’s address, directions, side effects and risks where relevant. This information helps a buyer compare products, use them safely and prove a complaint.

MRP means Maximum Retail Price. A seller must not charge more than it, though a buyer may bargain for less. The Right to Information Act, 2005 extends access to information about government departments.

### Waiting...

Amritha received no result after a government-job interview. She used the RTI Act and learned the reason for delay; she also received her appointment letter. The case shows how information can make a public authority answerable.

### LET’S WORK THESE OUT

### When choice is denied

The right to choose is denied when a seller ties one product or service to another—for example, selling toothpaste only with a toothbrush or giving a gas connection only if the buyer also purchases a stove.

### A Refund

Abirami paid two years’ coaching fees in advance but left after one year because teaching quality was poor. The District Commission ordered a refund; the State Commission upheld it and added compensation and cost. The case shows that a service user has a right to choose whether to continue.

### Where should consumers go to get justice?

Consumers have the right to seek redressal against unfair trade practices and receive compensation according to damage. A complaint may be filed before the appropriate Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission, with or without a lawyer. The textbook also notes online filing, class-action complaints and video-conference proceedings.

Consumer protection councils or forums guide and sometimes represent consumers. Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions are the quasi-judicial bodies that hear cases and issue orders. They are not the same institution.

### Prakash’s nine-step redressal visual

1. `PRAKASH GOES TO THE POST OFFICE TO SEND MONEY ORDER TO HIS DAUGHTER` — he buys a service.
2. `PRAKASH COMES TO KNOW THAT THE MONEY HAS NOT REACHED HIS DAUGHTER` — he identifies the service failure.
3. `PRAKASH ENQUIRES ABOUT THE MONEY ORDER IN THE POST OFFICE` — he first contacts the provider.
4. `THE POST OFFICE DOES NOT RESPOND TO THE QUERY SATISFACTORILY` — internal complaint does not solve it.
5. `PRAKASH GOES TO THE LOCAL CONSUMER PROTECTION COUNCIL FOR ADVICE` — a consumer body guides him.
6. `PRAKASH GOES TO A CONSUMER DISPUTES REDRESSAL COMMISSION TO FILE A CASE. HE FILLS A REGISTRATION FORM. THE COMMISSION SENDS NOTICE TO THE OTHER PARTY` — the formal redressal process begins.
7. `HE HIMSELF PLEADS THE CASE IN THE COMMISSION OFFICE` — a consumer need not always hire a lawyer.
8. `THE COMMISSION OFFICE JUDGE VERIFIES THE DOCUMENTS AND HEARS THE ARGUMENTS OF BOTH THE AGGRIEVED PARTY AND THE OTHER PARTY` — evidence and both sides are heard.
9. `THE JUDGE ANNOUNCES THE DISPUTES REDRESSAL COMMISSION VERDICT.` — the Commission gives its decision.

## LEARNING TO BECOME WELL-INFORMED CONSUMERS

Rights work when consumers know them, read labels, keep bills and warranties, compare claims and complain when necessary. The Consumer Protection Act led to Departments of Consumer Affairs and public-awareness material such as `Jago Grahak Jago`.

### ISI and Agmark

This special box explains that ISI, Agmark, Hallmark and `+F` logos help consumers identify products that meet stated quality standards. Standards are voluntary for many goods but mandatory for some goods affecting health and safety or used widely, such as LPG cylinders, food colours and additives, cement and packaged drinking water.

### Certification-logo visual and awareness posters

The printed visual gathers ISI, Agmark, Hallmark and `+F` marks so learners can recognise them. The consumer-awareness and environmental posters connect informed buying with rights, public campaigns and responsible action.

## TAKING THE CONSUMER MOVEMENT FORWARD

India observes 24 December as National Consumers’ Day because COPRA was enacted on that date in 1986. The movement has grown, but the textbook says only a small share of consumer groups are well organised.

Redressal can still be costly, slow and difficult. Bills are often not issued, evidence is hard to collect and many purchases are small. The 2019 amendment includes internet purchases, holds service providers and manufacturers responsible for deficiencies or defects, and encourages mediation.

Progress therefore depends on stronger enforcement and active consumer participation, not law alone.

## EXERCISES: Questions and answers

### 1. Why are rules and regulations required in the marketplace? Illustrate with a few examples.

Answer: Consumers are scattered and often have less information and power than sellers. Rules prevent underweight goods, adulteration, unsafe products, hidden charges, false claims and sale above MRP, and they provide a path to compensation.

### 2. What factors gave birth to the consumer movement in India? Trace its evolution.

Answer: Unfair trade, shortages, hoarding, black marketing, adulteration and the lack of legal protection caused dissatisfaction. The organised movement grew in the 1960s; groups spread awareness and monitored services in the 1970s; public pressure led to COPRA in 1986 and its amendment in 2019.

### 3. Explain the need for consumer consciousness by giving two examples.

Answer: Awareness helps buyers prevent harm and prove complaints. For example, checking an expiry date avoids unsafe medicine, and keeping a bill and warranty helps obtain repair, replacement or refund for a defective appliance.

### 4. Mention a few factors which cause exploitation of consumers.

Answer: Unequal information and bargaining power, few powerful producers, scattered consumers, weak enforcement, false advertising, artificial shortages, adulteration and missing bills can enable exploitation.

### 5. What is the rationale behind the enactment of Consumer Protection Act 1986?

Answer: It created legal protection and a redressal system for consumers facing defective goods, deficient services and unfair trade practices, and recognised important consumer rights.

### 6. Describe some of your duties as consumers if you visit a shopping complex in your locality.

Answer: Read labels and prices, check quantity, manufacture and expiry dates, look for required certification, ask for a bill, follow safe-use instructions, avoid misleading offers and complain with evidence when rights are denied.

### 7. Suppose you buy a bottle of honey and a biscuit packet. Which logo or mark you will have to look for and why?

Answer: Look for Agmark on honey and the applicable recognised standard mark, such as ISI/BIS, on the biscuit packet, along with complete label information. Such marks indicate conformity with stated quality standards.

### 8. What legal measures were taken by the government to empower the consumers in India?

Answer: The government enacted COPRA in 1986 and amended it in 2019, established district, state and national Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions, recognised consumer rights, enabled complaints and appeals, and supported standards and awareness programmes.

### 9. Mention some of the rights of consumers and write a few sentences on each.

Answer: Right to safety protects against hazardous goods and services. Right to information gives product and service details. Right to choice prevents forced tying. Right to representation lets consumer interests be heard. Right to redressal provides remedy or compensation. Right to consumer education helps people use all these rights.

### 10. By what means can the consumers express their solidarity?

Answer: They can form consumer groups, join campaigns, share reliable information, make collective or class-action complaints, support affected consumers and press businesses and government to enforce fair rules.

### 11. Critically examine the progress of consumer movement in India.

Answer: The movement secured legal rights, commissions, awareness campaigns and many consumer groups. Yet proceedings can be slow, expensive and evidence-heavy, many groups are weak, and enforcement remains uneven. Progress is real but incomplete.

### 12. Match the following. (i) Availing details of ingredients of a product (a) Right to safety (ii) Agmark (b) Dealing with consumer cases (iii) Accident due to faulty engine in a scooter (c) Certification of edible oil and cereals (iv) District Consumer Commission (d) Agency that develop standards for goods and services (v) Food fortification (e) Right to information (vi) Consumers International (f) Global level institution of consumer welfare organisations (vii) Bureau of Indian Standards (g) Addition of key nutrients to staple foods

Answer: (i)–(e), (ii)–(c), (iii)–(a), (iv)–(b), (v)–(g), (vi)–(f), (vii)–(d).

### 13. Say True or False. (i) COPRA applies only to goods. (ii) India is one of the many countries in the world which has exclusive authorities established for consumer disputes redressal. (iii) When a consumer feels that he has been exploited, he must file a case in the District Consumer Commission. (iv) It is worthwhile to move to consumer commissions only if the damages incurred are of high value. (v) Hallmark is the certification maintained for standardisation of jewellry. (vi) The consumer redressal process is very simple and quick. (vii) A consumer has the right to get compensation depending on the degree of the damage.

Answer: (i) False—services are covered too. (ii) True. (iii) False—the appropriate level and other redressal routes depend on the case. (iv) False—low-value claims also deserve remedy. (v) True. (vi) False—the textbook describes the process as often cumbersome and time-consuming. (vii) True.

## Common mistakes

- Treating consumer awareness as a substitute for seller responsibility and enforcement.
- Confusing a consumer protection council with a Consumer Disputes Redressal Commission.
- Saying MRP is the price a seller must charge; it is the maximum.
- Assuming a “free” offer is automatically beneficial.
- Filing a claim without preserving bills, warranty or communication records.

## Board tips

- In a case answer, name the denied right, supporting evidence and suitable remedy.
- Trace the movement in order: unfair practices → organised groups → COPRA → later strengthening.
- Distinguish safety, information, choice and redressal with separate examples.
- For “critically examine”, include both progress and remaining limitations; avoid predictions.

## Answer check

1. Right to choice.
2. It proves purchase, price, date and seller for a complaint.
3. A council guides or represents consumers; a Commission hears disputes and issues orders.
4. Extra quantity is certain and measurable, while a prize depends on chance and conditions.

## One-minute revision

- Consumers need protection because market power and information are unequal.
- The consumer movement grew from collective action against unfair practices.
- Core rights include safety, information, choice, representation, redressal and education.
- Bills, labels, warranties and recognised marks support informed decisions and complaints.
- Consumer groups guide people; Consumer Disputes Redressal Commissions decide cases.
- Law helps, but enforcement and active participation determine real progress.

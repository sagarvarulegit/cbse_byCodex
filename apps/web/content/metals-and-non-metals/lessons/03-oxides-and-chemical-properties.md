## General rule

`Metal + Oxygen → Metal oxide`

### Copper

Hot copper does not burn, but its brown surface becomes coated with black
copper(II) oxide:

`2Cu(s) + O₂(g) → 2CuO(s)`

### Aluminium

`4Al(s) + 3O₂(g) → 2Al₂O₃(s)`

### Magnesium

Magnesium burns with a dazzling white flame:

`2Mg(s) + O₂(g) → 2MgO(s)`

## Basic and amphoteric metal oxides

Most metal oxides are **basic** because they react with acids to make salt and
water.

Some oxides react with both acids and bases. They are **amphoteric oxides**.
The two NCERT examples are aluminium oxide and zinc oxide.

### Aluminium oxide with acid

`Al₂O₃(s) + 6HCl(aq) → 2AlCl₃(aq) + 3H₂O(l)`

### Aluminium oxide with base

`Al₂O₃(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → 2NaAlO₂(aq) + H₂O(l)`

The salt formed with sodium hydroxide is sodium aluminate.

For another amphoteric example:

`ZnO(s) + 2HCl(aq) → ZnCl₂(aq) + H₂O(l)`

`ZnO(s) + 2NaOH(aq) → Na₂ZnO₂(aq) + H₂O(l)`

## Oxides that form alkalis

Most metal oxides are insoluble in water. Sodium oxide and potassium oxide
dissolve in water and form soluble bases called alkalis:

`Na₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2NaOH(aq)`

`K₂O(s) + H₂O(l) → 2KOH(aq)`

## Reactivity evidence in air

| Metal | Textbook observation |
| --- | --- |
| Potassium and sodium | React so vigorously in air that they catch fire; stored in kerosene |
| Magnesium, aluminium, zinc and lead | Develop a thin protective oxide layer at ordinary temperature |
| Iron | Does not burn as a solid piece on heating; iron filings burn vigorously in a burner flame |
| Copper | Does not burn; hot surface forms black CuO |
| Silver and gold | Do not react with oxygen even at high temperature |

The thin oxide layer on magnesium, aluminium, zinc and lead limits further
oxidation. Therefore, “more reactive” does not always mean a metal object keeps
burning visibly.

## Do You Know — anodising

**Anodising** forms a thick protective oxide layer on aluminium.

1. Clean aluminium is made the **anode**.
2. It is electrolysed using dilute sulphuric acid.
3. Oxygen released at the anode reacts with aluminium.
4. A thicker aluminium oxide layer forms, improving corrosion resistance.
5. The layer can be dyed to give an attractive finish.

## High-scoring distinctions

### Basic oxide versus amphoteric oxide

- Basic oxide reacts with an acid to form salt and water.
- Amphoteric oxide reacts with **both an acid and a base** to form salt and
  water.

### Protective coating versus corrosion

The thin aluminium oxide coat blocks oxygen from the metal below. Anodising
deliberately makes this protective coat thicker.

## Common mistakes

- Writing `AlO` instead of `Al₂O₃`.
- Giving only one reaction when asked to prove that an oxide is amphoteric.
- Calling sodium aluminate sodium aluminium oxide without its formula.
- Saying copper burns with a black flame; the textbook says hot copper forms a
  black **surface layer** of CuO.

## One-minute revision

Metals form oxides with oxygen. Most metal oxides are basic, while Al₂O₃ and
ZnO are amphoteric. Reactivity ranges from sodium catching fire to gold not
reacting. Aluminium’s oxide coat protects it; anodising makes that coat thicker.

# Chapter 4: Agriculture

The same crop may feed a family, supply a factory or earn money in world trade. To understand Indian agriculture, connect farming type, season, crop conditions, region and reform.

## Types of Farming

Farming changes with the physical environment, technology and socio-cultural practices. It may range from subsistence production for a family to commercial production for markets and industry.

## Primitive Subsistence Farming

Primitive subsistence farming uses small patches of land, simple tools and family or community labour. It depends on monsoon, natural soil fertility and local environmental conditions.

Slash-and-burn cultivation clears a patch, grows crops and then leaves it so natural fertility can recover. The textbook lists local names such as *jhumming* in the north-east, *bewar* or *dahiya* in Madhya Pradesh, *podu* in Andhra Pradesh, *pama dabi* in Odisha and *khil* in the Himalayan belt.

## Intensive Subsistence Farming

This labour-intensive farming is common in densely populated areas. Farmers use irrigation and biochemical inputs to obtain more from limited land. Small holdings often result from repeated division through inheritance.

## Commercial Farming

Commercial farming uses modern inputs such as high-yielding-variety seeds, chemical fertilisers, insecticides and pesticides. The degree of commercialisation differs by region; rice may be commercial in Punjab and Haryana but mainly subsistence in Odisha.

Plantation farming grows one crop over a large area and links agriculture with industry. It is capital-intensive, uses migrant labour and requires good transport. Tea, coffee, rubber, sugarcane and banana are examples.

## Cropping Pattern

| Season | Sowing and harvest | Main examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rabi | Sown October-December; harvested April-June | Wheat, barley, peas, gram and mustard |
| Kharif | Sown with monsoon; harvested September-October | Paddy, maize, jowar, bajra, tur, moong, urad, cotton, jute, groundnut and soyabean |
| Zaid | Short summer season between rabi and kharif | Watermelon, muskmelon, cucumber, vegetables and fodder crops |

## Major Crops

### Rice

Rice is a kharif crop needing high temperature above 25 degrees Celsius, high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm. Irrigation allows it in lower-rainfall regions. Major areas include northern and north-eastern plains, coastal regions and deltaic areas.

### Wheat

Wheat is the main rabi cereal in north and north-west India. It needs a cool growing season, bright sunshine while ripening and about 50-75 cm rainfall. The Ganga-Satluj plains and the black-soil region of the Deccan are important zones.

### Millets and maize

Jowar, bajra and ragi are coarse grains with high nutritional value. Ragi is rich in iron, calcium and other nutrients. Maize is used as food and fodder and usually grows as a kharif crop between about 21 and 27 degrees Celsius.

### Pulses

Tur, urad, moong, masur, peas and gram are major protein sources. Most are leguminous and fix nitrogen, so they help restore soil fertility when rotated with other crops.

## Food Crops other than Grains

Sugarcane needs a hot and humid climate, about 21-27 degrees Celsius and 75-100 cm rainfall, with irrigation in drier regions. India produces sugar, gur, khandsari and molasses.

Oilseeds include groundnut, mustard, coconut, sesamum, soyabean, castor, cottonseed, linseed and sunflower. Tea is a labour-intensive plantation crop that needs warm, moist, frost-free conditions and well-drained humus-rich soil. Coffee grows mainly in Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. Horticulture includes fruits, vegetables and flowers.

## Non-Food Crops

Rubber needs moist, humid conditions, over 200 cm rainfall and temperature above 25 degrees Celsius. Fibre crops include cotton, jute, hemp and natural silk.

Cotton needs high temperature, light rainfall or irrigation, 210 frost-free days and bright sunshine. It grows well on black soil. Jute grows on well-drained fertile flood-plain soil renewed each year and needs high temperature.

## Technological and Institutional Reforms

After Independence, land reform, consolidation of holdings, cooperation and abolition of zamindari received priority. Later measures included the Green Revolution, White Revolution, crop insurance, rural banks, cooperatives, Kisan Credit Cards, Personal Accident Insurance Scheme, weather bulletins and minimum support prices.

These reforms aim to raise production, improve credit and reduce farmers' risk. Benefits are not automatic; access, information and fair implementation matter.

### Bhoodan-Gramdan

Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan movement asked landowners to donate land to landless villagers. Gramdan extended the idea to villages. The movement became known as a blood-less revolution.

## NCERT exercise questions and answers

### 1. Multiple choice questions

**(i) Which one of the following describes a system of agriculture where a single crop is grown on a large area?**

*Answer:* (b) Plantation Agriculture.

**(ii) Which one of the following is a rabi crop?**

*Answer:* (b) Gram.

**(iii) Which one of the following is a leguminous crop?**

*Answer:* (a) Pulses.

### 2. Answer the following questions in 30 words

**(i) Name one important beverage crop and specify the geographical conditions required for its growth.**

*Answer:* Tea needs a warm, moist, frost-free climate, frequent showers and deep, fertile, well-drained soil rich in humus. Tender leaves also require much skilled labour.

**(ii) Name one staple crop of India and the regions where it is produced.**

*Answer:* Rice is a major staple. It is grown in northern and north-eastern plains, coastal and deltaic regions, and irrigated parts of Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan.

**(iii) Enlist the various institutional reform programmes introduced by the government in the interest of farmers.**

*Answer:* Land reform, consolidation, cooperatives, crop insurance, rural credit, Kisan Credit Cards, accident insurance, weather bulletins and minimum support prices are key programmes.

### 3. Answer the following questions in about 120 words

**(i) Suggest the initiative taken by the government to ensure the increase in agricultural production.**

*Answer:* Government initiatives include land reform, consolidation of fragmented holdings and encouragement of cooperatives. The Green Revolution introduced high-yielding seeds, irrigation and other inputs, while the White Revolution supported dairying. Rural banks, cooperative societies and Kisan Credit Cards improve access to credit. Crop insurance reduces risk from drought, flood, cyclone, fire and disease. Weather bulletins, farm programmes and minimum support prices provide information and income support. These measures work best when small farmers can access them fairly and use water and soil sustainably.

**(ii) Describe the geographical conditions required for the growth of rice.**

*Answer:* Rice needs high temperature above 25 degrees Celsius, high humidity and annual rainfall above 100 cm. It grows well on fertile alluvial or clayey soil that can hold water. Level fields and a reliable supply of water support flooded cultivation. It is therefore common in the northern and north-eastern plains, coastal areas and river deltas. Irrigation also permits rice in lower-rainfall regions such as Punjab, Haryana, western Uttar Pradesh and parts of Rajasthan.

## Common mistakes

- Calling every large farm a plantation without the single-crop and industry link.
- Mixing rabi sowing time with kharif sowing time.
- Listing a crop without its temperature, rainfall or soil condition.
- Treating millets as nutritionally inferior.
- Describing reforms as technology only and forgetting credit, insurance and land measures.

## One-minute revision

- Primitive subsistence: simple tools and local use.
- Intensive subsistence: much labour on limited land.
- Commercial: modern inputs and market production.
- Rabi in winter, kharif with monsoon, zaid in summer.
- Crop answer = season + climate + soil + region.
- Reforms join land, technology, credit, insurance and price support.

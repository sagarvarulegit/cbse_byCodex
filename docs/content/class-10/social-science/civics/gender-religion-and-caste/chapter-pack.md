# Chapter 3: Gender, Religion and Caste

## Hook

Social differences do not disappear when people enter a democracy. The important question is what politics does with them: does it challenge unfairness and give excluded people a voice, or does it turn identity into domination and conflict?

**Chapter goal:** Understand how gender, religion and caste create divisions and inequalities in India, and judge when their political expression supports equality and when it harms democracy.

## Overview

Social diversity need not threaten democracy. Political expression of social differences can even be desirable. This chapter studies gender, religion and caste: the form each division takes in India, how each enters politics, and whether its political expression is healthy or harmful.

## Gender and politics

Gender division is a hierarchical social division found widely across societies. It is often treated as natural, but it grows mainly from social expectations and stereotypes, not biology. Politics matters because unequal gender roles affect power, recognition, rights and opportunities.

## Public/private division

Society often teaches children that women should manage housework and child care while men should work outside. This **sexual division of labour** is not based on ability:

- Men work as paid tailors and hotel cooks, showing that they can do work often labelled “women’s work” at home.
- Rural women fetch water, collect fuel and work in fields. Urban women work as domestic helpers and in offices.
- Many women do paid work as well as unpaid household labour, but much of their work is not recognised or valued.

Women were once largely excluded from voting, public office and public affairs. Women organised for equal political and legal rights, better education and careers, and equality in family life. These are **feminist movements**. In Scandinavian countries such as Sweden, Norway and Finland, women’s participation in public life became very high.

India remains a patriarchal society despite improvements:

- The textbook gives women’s literacy as **54 per cent**, compared with **76 per cent** for men, and notes that fewer girls reach higher education.
- Women are under-represented in highly paid and valued jobs.
- The Equal Remuneration Act, 1976 says equal work should receive equal wages, yet women are often paid less for the same work.
- Much household work is unpaid and invisible. On average, an Indian woman works one hour more each day than an average man.
- Sex-selective abortion lowered the child sex ratio to **919 girls per 1,000 boys** in the discussion.
- Women face harassment, exploitation, domestic violence and unsafe public spaces.

## Women’s political representation

Women’s issues often receive too little attention. Feminists therefore argue that women need a stronger share of political power.

- Women were only **14.36 per cent** of Lok Sabha members in 2019, and their share in State Assemblies was below **5 per cent**.
- One-third of seats in Panchayats and Municipalities are reserved for women. The chapter reports more than **10 lakh** elected women representatives in rural and urban local bodies.
- Women’s organisations long demanded at least one-third reservation in the Lok Sabha and State Assemblies.
- The **Nari Shakti Vandan Adhiniyam (Women’s Reservation Act, 2023)** has been passed and will provide 33 per cent reservation for women in the Lok Sabha, State Legislative Assemblies and the Delhi Assembly.

Raising gender inequality in politics helped women gain rights and representation. Unlike an exclusive claim that one group should dominate, feminism seeks equal treatment.

## Religion, communalism and politics

Religious diversity is widespread. Religion and politics are not always a dangerous combination:

- Gandhiji said politics should be guided by ethical values found in religions, not by one particular religion.
- Human-rights groups ask governments to prevent communal riots and protect religious minorities.
- Women’s movements ask governments to reform discriminatory family laws.
- People may express the needs and interests of a religious community, and the state may regulate religious practice to prevent discrimination, as long as every religion is treated equally.

### Textbook source guide

- **Unnumbered student cartoon prompt:** “I am not religious. Why should I bother about communalism and secularism?” Communalism affects equal citizenship and national life, so it matters even to a person without a religion.
- **Glossary box — “Family laws”:** Laws dealing with marriage, divorce, adoption, inheritance and other family matters. India has different family laws for followers of different religions.

## Communalism

Communal politics begins when religion is treated as the main basis of the nation and one religious group is placed against another. It assumes that followers of one religion form a single community with the same interests and that followers of different religions cannot share a social community. This is flawed because every person has several identities and every religious community contains many voices.

Communalism appears in four main forms:

1. **Everyday beliefs:** religious prejudice, stereotypes and a belief that one religion is superior.
2. **Quest for political dominance:** a majority community may seek majoritarian rule, while a minority may seek a separate political unit.
3. **Political mobilisation on religious lines:** sacred symbols, religious leaders, emotions and fear are used to gather voters of one religion.
4. **Communal violence:** riots and massacres, including the violence at Partition and later communal conflicts.

### Textbook source guide

- **Unnumbered student cartoon prompt:** “I often crack jokes about people from one religion. Does that make me communal?” Repeated jokes can carry prejudice and stereotypes, the everyday form of communalism, even when the speaker does not notice it.

## Secular state

India’s Constitution chose a secular state to meet the challenge of communalism:

- India has no official religion; the Constitution gives no religion a special status.
- Every person and community is free to profess, practise and propagate any religion, or to follow none.
- The Constitution prohibits discrimination on religious grounds.
- The state may intervene in religion to ensure equality within a religious community; banning untouchability is the example given.

A secular Constitution is necessary but not sufficient. Citizens must also challenge communal prejudice and propaganda in everyday life and oppose religion-based mobilisation in politics.

## Caste and politics

Caste division is special to India. Many societies pass occupations from one generation to another, but the traditional caste system made hereditary occupations a ritual rule. People were expected to marry within their caste and avoid eating with other castes. “Outcaste” groups suffered exclusion and untouchability.

Reformers such as Jotiba Phule, Gandhiji, B. R. Ambedkar and Periyar Ramaswami Naicker worked against caste inequality. Economic development, urbanisation, literacy, education, occupational mobility and weaker landlord power have broken down old caste hierarchies in many ways. The Constitution bans caste discrimination and policies try to reverse historical injustice.

## Caste inequalities

Caste has not disappeared:

- Most people still marry within their caste or tribe.
- Untouchability has not ended completely despite its constitutional ban.
- Groups that historically received education gained an advantage in modern education and urban middle-class positions; groups denied education often lagged behind.
- Caste remains linked with economic status, although rich and poor people now exist in every caste.

## Caste in politics

Caste can enter electoral politics in several ways:

- Parties consider a constituency’s caste composition and nominate candidates from different castes.
- Parties and candidates appeal to caste feelings for support; some are seen as representing particular castes.
- Universal adult franchise and one-person-one-vote made leaders seek support from communities earlier treated as inferior.
- Governments usually try to include representatives of different castes and tribes.

But elections are not only about caste:

- No parliamentary constituency has a clear majority of one caste, so candidates need support from more than one community.
- No party receives every vote of one caste. A “vote bank” only means a large proportion may support one party.
- Parties and sitting MPs or MLAs often lose, which would not happen if caste preferences were fixed.
- Party attachment, economic position, gender, government performance and leader popularity also shape voting.

### Textbook source guide

- **Cartoon — “THE VOTE BANK OF INDIA”:** The caption asks: “Do you think that political leaders are right to treat people belonging to a caste as ‘vote banks’?” The chapter’s answer is no: people within a caste have different interests and do not vote as a single fixed block.

## Politics in caste

Politics also changes caste identities:

- A caste group may expand by including neighbouring castes or sub-castes.
- Caste groups form coalitions with other castes and communities, creating dialogue and negotiation.
- Broader political categories such as “backward” and “forward” caste groups emerge.

This can help disadvantaged communities demand dignity, land, resources, opportunity and a share in decision-making. But politics based only on caste identity can hide poverty, development and corruption, and can create tension, conflict or violence. The democratic task is to use political voice against inequality without making caste the only identity.

## Exercises

### 1. Mention different aspects of life in which women are discriminated or disadvantaged in India.

**Answer:** Women face unequal access to education and highly paid work, lower wages for equal work, an unpaid household burden, sex-selective abortion and a low child sex ratio, violence and harassment, and very low representation in legislatures and cabinets.

### 2. State different forms of communal politics with one example each.

**Answer:** Communal politics appears as (i) everyday prejudice, such as stereotypes about another religion; (ii) a quest for dominance, such as majority-community rule; (iii) religious mobilisation, such as asking for votes through religious symbols or fear; and (iv) communal violence, such as the riots at Partition.

### 3. State how caste inequalities are still continuing in India.

**Answer:** Many people still marry within caste; untouchability survives in some places; old advantages in education and resources continue; and the textbook’s poverty evidence shows Dalits and Adivasis are more likely to be poor while upper castes are over-represented among the rich.

### 4. State two reasons to say that caste alone cannot determine election results in India.

**Answer:** No parliamentary constituency has a majority of only one caste, so parties need several communities. Also, no party gets all votes of one caste, and ruling parties or sitting representatives frequently lose because party loyalty, economic interests, performance and leadership also matter.

### 5. What is the status of women’s representation in India’s legislative bodies?

**Answer:** It remains low. Women reached 14.36 per cent of Lok Sabha members in 2019, while their share in State Assemblies was below 5 per cent. One-third of positions in local bodies are reserved for women, and the 2023 law will provide 33 per cent reservation in the Lok Sabha, State Assemblies and Delhi Assembly.

### 6. Mention any two constitutional provisions that make India a secular state.

**Answer:** India has no official religion, and every person has freedom to profess, practise and propagate any religion or follow none. The Constitution also bans religious discrimination and permits intervention to ensure equality within communities.

## Common mistakes

- Treating sexual division of labour as a biological rule. The chapter describes it as socially created.
- Saying that every connection between religion and politics is communal. Equal treatment, ethics and protection from discrimination are legitimate concerns.
- Calling feminism the reverse domination of men. Feminism seeks equal rights and opportunities.
- Claiming that caste has disappeared, or that caste alone decides every election. Both extremes contradict the chapter.
- Confusing a secular state with a state that is hostile to religion. A secular state protects freedom and treats religions equally.

## Board tips

- For each social division, use the pattern **inequality → political expression → democratic benefit or danger**.
- In a question on communalism, name all four forms and attach one short example to each.
- Use at least two textbook facts when explaining women’s disadvantage or representation.
- In caste-and-politics answers, show the two-way relation: caste influences politics, and politics reshapes caste.

## One-minute revision

Gender roles are socially created and place unpaid work, fewer opportunities and low political representation on women. Feminist politics seeks equality. Religion may enter politics through ethics and equal-rights claims, but communalism turns religion into prejudice, dominance, mobilisation or violence. India is secular because it has no official religion and protects equal religious freedom. Caste inequality has weakened but survives; caste shapes politics, while political competition also reshapes caste identities.

# Chapter 4: Political Parties

## Hook

Suppose every election candidate stood alone, with no shared programme and no team responsible for running the country. Voters might choose individuals, but who would gather different views, form a stable government, make policies and accept responsibility for national results?

**Chapter goal:** Understand what political parties are, why representative democracy needs them, how party systems and recognised parties work in India, and how democratic participation can address the main challenges within parties.

## Overview

Political parties have shaped the rise of democracy, constitutional design, elections, governments, federal power-sharing and social divisions. This chapter asks why parties are needed and how many are suitable, introduces national and State parties in India, and examines their problems and possible reforms.

## Why do we need political parties?

Political parties are among democracy’s most visible institutions. Even people who know little about the Constitution may know a party. Visibility does not mean popularity: parties are often blamed for political and social divisions. To judge whether they are needed, we must first understand their meaning and functions.

## Meaning

A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government. They agree on policies and programmes meant to promote what they see as the collective good. Because people disagree about the best policies, parties represent different parts, views and interests of society.

A party has three components:

- leaders;
- active members; and
- followers.

**Key term — Partisan:** A person strongly committed to a party, group or faction. Partisanship means taking a side and often being unable to take a balanced view.

## Functions

Political parties perform seven linked functions:

1. **Contest elections:** Parties choose candidates. In some countries members and supporters choose them; in India, top party leaders generally make the choice.
2. **Offer policies and programmes:** Parties group many opinions into a few positions, giving voters manageable choices and governments a policy direction.
3. **Make laws:** Legislators usually belong to parties and often follow party leadership when laws are debated and passed.
4. **Form and run governments:** Parties recruit and train leaders, make them ministers and direct major policy decisions.
5. **Act as opposition:** Parties that lose question the government, criticise failures and wrong policies, and mobilise public opposition.
6. **Shape public opinion:** Parties raise issues, organise movements and help opinions form around public questions.
7. **Connect people with government:** Citizens often approach local party leaders for government machinery and welfare schemes because they are easier to reach than officials.

Parties must remain responsive because voters can reject them in the next election.

## Necessity

Imagine elections without parties. Every candidate would be independent. Representatives might answer for local work, but no organised group would promise major national policies or accept responsibility for how the whole country is run.

Even formally non-party Panchayat elections often split a village into groups that put up panels of candidates—almost the same work a party performs. Large representative democracies need agencies that collect different views, bring representatives together, support or restrain government, make policies, and justify or oppose them. Political parties are therefore a necessary condition for representative democracy.

## How many parties should we have?

India has more than **750** parties registered with the Election Commission in the chapter’s account, but only a smaller number seriously competes for power.

- **One-party system:** Only one party controls and runs government. China is the example. This is not a democratic option because there is no fair competition for power.
- **Two-party system:** Two main parties have a serious chance of forming government, although smaller parties may exist. The USA and United Kingdom are examples.
- **Multiparty system:** Several parties can come to power alone or in alliance. India has this system.

When parties join to contest elections and win power, they form an **alliance** or **front**. The chapter names the National Democratic Alliance, United Progressive Alliance and Left Front from the 2004 Lok Sabha election. A multiparty system can look messy and unstable, but it represents many interests and opinions.

No country simply chooses its ideal party system. It develops from social and regional divisions, political history and its election system. India’s great social and geographical diversity is not easily absorbed by two or three parties, so a multiparty system evolved.

## National parties

India has parties active mainly in one State and parties with units across several or all States. The latter follow national policies, programmes and strategy. Every party registers with the Election Commission. Recognised parties receive benefits such as a reserved election symbol.

The chapter states that, under the Election Commission notification issued in **2023**, there are **six recognised national parties**:

- **Aam Aadmi Party (AAP):** Formed on 26 November 2012 after the 2011 anti-corruption movement. It stresses accountability, clean administration, transparency and good governance. It formed governments in Delhi and Punjab and won one Lok Sabha seat in 2019.
- **Bahujan Samaj Party (BSP):** Formed in 1984 under Kanshi Ram. It seeks power and welfare for the bahujan samaj—Dalits, Adivasis, OBCs and religious minorities—and draws on Sahu Maharaj, Mahatma Phule, Periyar Ramaswami Naicker and Babasaheb Ambedkar. Its main base is Uttar Pradesh. In 2019 it polled about 3.63 per cent and won 10 Lok Sabha seats.
- **Bharatiya Janata Party (BJP):** Founded in 1980 by reviving the Bharatiya Jana Sangh, formed by Syama Prasad Mukherjee in 1951. It draws on ancient Indian culture, integral humanism and Antyodaya; cultural nationalism or Hindutva is important to its idea of nationhood. It expanded beyond northern and urban support and won 303 Lok Sabha seats in 2019, leading the NDA government at the Centre.
- **Communist Party of India–Marxist (CPI-M):** Founded in 1964. It believes in Marxism–Leninism, supports socialism, secularism and democracy, and opposes imperialism and communalism. It has support in West Bengal, Kerala and Tripura, especially among workers, farmers, labourers, poor people and the intelligentsia. It won about 1.75 per cent of votes and three seats in 2019.
- **Indian National Congress (INC):** Founded in 1885 and commonly called the Congress Party. It dominated national and State politics for decades after Independence. Under Jawaharlal Nehru it sought a modern, secular, democratic republic. It is centrist, supports secularism and weaker sections, and accepts economic reforms “with a human face.” Its printed profile calls it the leader of the United Progressive Alliance (UPA) government from 2004 to 2019. It won 19.5 per cent of votes and 52 seats in 2019.
- **National People’s Party (NPP):** Formed in July 2013 under P. A. Sangma. It was the first party from North-East India to gain national-party status. It stresses regional diversity, education, employment and empowerment, formed government in Meghalaya, and won one Lok Sabha seat in 2019.

## State parties

Most other major parties are recognised as State parties and are often called regional parties. A State party need not have a regional ideology: some have organisations in several States but electoral success in only a few. The chapter names the Samajwadi Party and Rashtriya Janata Dal as national-level organisations with units in several States, and Biju Janata Dal, Sikkim Democratic Front, Mizo National Front and Telangana Rashtra Samithi as parties conscious of State identity.

State parties grew in number and strength over three decades. Until 2014, no national party could secure a Lok Sabha majority on its own. From 1996, nearly every State party had a chance to join a national coalition. This made Parliament more diverse and strengthened federalism and democracy.

## Challenges to political parties

Parties face four major challenges:

1. **Lack of internal democracy:** Power concentrates in one or a few leaders. Parties may not keep membership registers, hold meetings or conduct internal elections. Ordinary members lack information and influence, and personal loyalty becomes more important than principles.
2. **Dynastic succession:** When procedures are not open, leaders can favour relatives. Capable ordinary workers have fewer chances to rise, and people without enough experience or support may gain power.
3. **Money and muscle power:** Parties focused on winning may choose wealthy candidates, depend on rich donors or support criminals who can win. Donors can influence policy.
4. **No meaningful choice:** Parties may differ little in ideology or policy, and leaders may shift from one party to another, leaving voters without a real alternative.

## How can parties be reformed?

### Measures already taken

- The Constitution was amended against **defection**. An MLA or MP who changes party after election loses the seat. This reduced defection but also made disagreement with party leaders harder.
- The Supreme Court required election candidates to file an **affidavit** giving property details and pending criminal cases. This increased public information, although truth-checking remains weak.
- The Election Commission required parties to hold organisational elections and file income-tax returns. Parties often follow these as formalities, so their effect on internal democracy is uncertain.

### Suggestions discussed in the chapter

- Make a law requiring parties to keep membership registers, follow their constitutions, use an independent authority for disputes, and hold open elections for top posts.
- Require about one-third of party tickets and decision-making positions to go to women.
- Provide State funding for elections, in cash or in kind, based on a fair rule such as previous vote share.

Legal rules alone can be counter-productive if parties find ways around them. Two broader democratic paths are also needed:

- citizens, movements, pressure groups and media can use petitions, publicity and agitations to create reform pressure; and
- people who want better parties can join them and participate from within.

The quality of democracy depends on public participation: bad politics is solved by more and better politics.

### 1. State the various functions political parties perform in a democracy.

**Answer:** Parties contest elections; offer policies and programmes; make laws; form and run governments; act as opposition; shape public opinion; and connect people with government machinery and welfare schemes.

### 2. What are the various challenges faced by political parties?

**Answer:** The four challenges are lack of internal democracy, dynastic succession, the growing role of money and muscle power, and failure to offer voters a meaningful political choice.

### 3. Suggest some reforms to strengthen parties so that they perform their functions well?

**Answer:** Parties can keep genuine membership registers, hold regular open internal elections, disclose candidates’ property and criminal cases, file financial returns, give women at least one-third of tickets and decision posts, consider fair State funding, and respond to citizen pressure. Citizens can also join parties and improve them from within.

**Answer:** A political party is a group of people who come together to contest elections and hold power in government, agreeing on certain policies and programmes for society.

**Answer:** A party seeks political power through elections, follows shared policies and programmes, represents a part or viewpoint of society, and includes leaders, active members and followers.

## Common mistakes

- Describing parties only as election machines and forgetting law-making, opposition, public opinion and citizen access.
- Calling every multiparty system a coalition government. A coalition exists when parties actually join to govern.
- Treating a State party as necessarily regional in ideology. Recognition mainly describes electoral strength and presence.
- Mixing dynastic succession with defection. One concerns family control; the other is changing party after election.
- Listing legal reforms without noting that public pressure and participation inside parties are also needed.

## One-minute revision

A political party contests elections and seeks government power through shared policies. Parties perform seven essential representative functions. One-party systems deny fair competition; two-party systems centre on two main contenders; multiparty systems give several parties or alliances a chance. India’s national and State parties make Parliament diverse and support federalism. Parties need reform because of centralised leadership, dynasties, money and muscle, and weak voter choice; law, public pressure and active participation must work together.

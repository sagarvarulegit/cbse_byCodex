# Chapter 5: Outcomes of Democracy

## Hook

Democracy promises voice and equality, but citizens still see delays, corruption, poverty and conflict. Should democracy be judged only by whether every problem disappears, or by the conditions it creates, the rights it protects and the way people can demand improvement?

**Chapter goal:** Learn how to assess democracy fairly and examine its record in government quality, economic development, inequality, social diversity, dignity and freedom.

## Overview

This final chapter asks what democracy does and what outcomes can reasonably be expected from it. It compares expectations with actual performance in quality of government, economic well-being, inequality, social conflict, freedom and dignity.

## How do we assess democracy’s outcomes?

Democracy is preferred because it:

- promotes equality among citizens;
- enhances individual dignity;
- improves the quality of decision-making;
- provides a method to resolve conflict; and
- allows room to correct mistakes.

People strongly support democracy as a principle but are often dissatisfied with its practice. Democracies also differ greatly in their society, economy and culture, so their achievements differ.

The first rule of fair assessment is that democracy is a **form of government**, not a machine that solves every social, economic and political problem by itself. It creates conditions in which goals can be pursued; citizens must use those conditions to achieve them. We should therefore identify reasonable democratic expectations and then check the evidence.

## Accountable, responsive and legitimate government

The most basic expected outcome is a government that is:

- **accountable** to citizens;
- **responsive** to their needs and expectations; and
- **legitimate**, because people choose it and regard it as their own.

Democratic decision-making follows norms and procedures. Citizens should be able to inspect how a decision was made—this is **transparency**—and participate when needed. Deliberation and negotiation can make decisions slower than a dictator’s order, but a decision reached through accepted procedures may be more acceptable and effective.

To measure accountability, look for regular, free and fair elections; open debate on policies and laws; and citizens’ right to government information. Democracies have a mixed record. They usually perform better than non-democratic regimes in these areas, but may still fail to give everyone a fair electoral chance, debate every decision, share enough information, meet people’s needs, or remain free of corruption. There is no evidence that non-democracies are necessarily less corrupt or more responsive.

Democracy’s strongest advantage is legitimacy. Even when democratic government is slow or imperfect, people prefer rule by elected representatives. Its ability to create support for itself is an important outcome.

## Economic growth and development

Democracy does not automatically guarantee economic development. Comparing all regimes from **1950 to 2000**, dictatorships had a slightly higher average growth rate. But among poor countries, the difference between democracy and dictatorship was negligible.

Economic development also depends on population size, the world situation, cooperation from other countries and national economic priorities. Growth alone is therefore not a sound reason to reject democracy. With its other positive outcomes, democracy should at least be expected not to lag behind dictatorship.

## Reduction of inequality and poverty

Democracy is built on political equality: each citizen’s vote has equal weight. Yet political equality can exist beside growing economic inequality.

A small number of very rich people may receive a disproportionate share of wealth and income, while people at the bottom struggle for food, clothing, housing, education and health. Although poor people form a large part of the electorate, democratic governments have not been very successful in reducing poverty and economic inequality. In some poor countries, people even depend on rich countries for food supplies.

This is a serious shortcoming, but it does not prove that dictatorship serves the poor better. It shows that citizens must use democratic power to demand fairer policies.

## Accommodation of social diversity

Democracy is especially suited to handling social differences through peaceful competition and negotiation. It cannot permanently remove every conflict, but it can prevent tensions from becoming explosive and violent.

Two conditions are necessary:

1. Majority rule must include cooperation with the minority so that government represents the general view. Majority and minority opinions are not permanent.
2. Rule by majority must never become rule by a permanent religious, racial or linguistic majority community. In each decision or election, different people and groups must have a real chance to form a majority.

Belgium met these conditions through accommodation; Sri Lanka’s majoritarian experience shows what happens when they are ignored.

## Dignity and freedom of the citizens

Democracy is superior to other forms of government in promoting individual dignity and freedom. Every person wants respect, and democracy recognises equal treatment at least as a principle.

Historically male-dominated societies did not automatically become equal, but democratic recognition gives women a legal and moral basis to challenge disrespect. The same is true for disadvantaged castes in India. Caste inequality and atrocities still occur, but they no longer have legitimate legal or moral foundations, and excluded groups can demand equal status and opportunity.

Democracy’s examination never ends. Each gain creates new expectations. Complaints can actually be evidence of democratic success because people have become citizens who critically judge power-holders rather than subjects who simply obey. Many people believe their vote can affect government and their own interests.

## Exercises

### 1. How does democracy produce an accountable, responsive and legitimate government?

**Answer:** Elections let people choose and replace rulers; public debate, procedures and the right to information let citizens examine decisions; and participation makes government answerable to needs. Because rulers are elected through accepted procedures, people regard democratic government as their own, giving it legitimacy.

### 2. What are the conditions under which democracies accommodate social diversities?

**Answer:** The majority must work with the minority so government represents the general view, and majority rule must not become permanent rule by one religious, racial or linguistic community. Every citizen and group must have a real chance to join a majority in different decisions or elections.

### 3. Give arguments to support or oppose the following assertions:

- Industrialised countries can afford democracy but the poor need dictatorship to become rich.
- Democracy can’t reduce inequality of incomes between different citizens.
- Government in poor countries should spend less on poverty reduction, health, education and spend more on industries and infrastructure.
- In democracy all citizens have one vote, which means that there is absence of any domination and conflict.

**Answer:**

- **Oppose:** Poor democracies and poor dictatorships show nearly the same growth rate in the textbook, while democracy also protects accountability, dignity and freedom.
- **Oppose the absolute claim:** Democracies have often failed to reduce inequality, but citizens can organise and demand redistributive policies; inequality is a performance problem, not proof that reduction is impossible.
- **Oppose:** Health, education and poverty reduction build human well-being and fair opportunity. Industrial spending without these can deepen inequality.
- **Oppose:** One vote gives political equality, but economic, caste, gender and community domination can continue. Democracy provides methods to challenge, not instantly erase, conflict.

### 4. Identify the challenges to democracy in the following descriptions. Also suggest policy/institutional mechanism to deepen democracy in the given situations:

- Following a High Court directive, a temple in Orissa that had separate entry doors for dalits and non-dalits allowed entry for all from the same door.
- A large number of farmers are committing suicide in different states of India.
- Following an allegation of killing of three civilians in Gandwara in a fake encounter by Jammu and Kashmir police, an enquiry has been ordered.

**Answer:**

- **Caste discrimination and dignity:** Enforce equal-entry and anti-untouchability law, monitor compliance and provide an accessible complaints process.
- **Economic inequality and rural distress:** Provide fair institutional credit, crop support and insurance, effective relief, and accountable rural-development measures shaped with farmers.
- **Police accountability and right to life:** Conduct an independent, transparent and time-bound enquiry, apply judicial and human-rights oversight, and punish wrongdoing through due process.

### 5. In the context of democracies, which of the following ideas is correct– democracies have successfully eliminated:

A. conflicts among people

B. economic inequalities among people

C. differences of opinion about how marginalised sections are to be treated

D. the idea of political inequality

**Answer:** **D. the idea of political inequality.** Democracy accepts equal political status, though other inequalities and conflicts remain.

### 6. In the context of assessing democracy, which among the following is the odd one out. Democracies need to ensure:

A. free and fair elections

B. dignity of the individual

C. majority rule

D. equal treatment before law

**Answer:** **C. majority rule.** The simple phrase is incomplete because a democratic majority must work with minorities and must not become permanent majority-community rule.

### 7. Studies on political and social inequalities in democracy show that:

A. democracy and development go together.

B. inequalities exist in democracies.

C. inequalities do not exist under dictatorship.

D. dictatorship is better than democracy.

**Answer:** **B. inequalities exist in democracies.**

### 8. Read the passage below:

> Nannu is a daily wage earner. He lives in Welcome Mazdoor Colony, a slum habitation in East Delhi. He lost his ration card and applied for a duplicate one in January 2004. He made several rounds to the local Food and Civil Supplies office for the next three months. But the clerks and officials would not even look at him, leave alone do his job or bother to tell him the status of his application. Ultimately, he filed an application under the Right to Information Act asking for the daily progress made on his application, names of the officials, who were supposed to act on his application and what action would be taken against these officials for their inaction. Within a week of filing application under the Right to Information Act, he was visited by an inspector from the Food Department, who informed him that the card had been made and he could collect it from the office. When Nannu went to collect his card next day, he was given a very warm treatment by the Food and Supply Officer (FSO), who is the head of a Circle. The FSO offered him tea and requested him to withdraw his application under the Right to Information, since his work had already been done.

What does Nannu’s example show? What impact did Nannu’s action have on officials? Ask your parents their experiences when they approach government officials to attend to their problems.

**Answer:** Nannu’s case shows that the Right to Information can make administration transparent and accountable and can empower even a poor citizen. His questions created a written trail, identified responsible officials and exposed delay, so the office acted within a week and treated him respectfully. The final part is a personal activity: record a parent’s real experience, including the office approached, delay or response, and whether information or follow-up improved the result.

## Common mistakes

- Expecting democracy alone to solve every social and economic problem without citizen action.
- Calling every delay proof of inefficiency. Deliberation can take time but may make a decision more acceptable and effective.
- Claiming democracies are free of corruption or economic inequality. The chapter describes a mixed record.
- Treating majority rule as permanent domination by one community.
- Assuming complaints mean democracy has failed. Informed public criticism can show that citizens expect accountability.

## Board tips

- Assess each outcome as **reasonable expectation → actual record → balanced conclusion**.
- Do not answer economic questions with only growth; discuss distribution and other democratic outcomes.
- For social diversity, write both textbook conditions clearly.
- In dignity answers, connect recognition of equality with the legal and moral power to struggle against discrimination.

## One-minute revision

Democracy creates conditions for citizens to pursue goals; it does not guarantee every result. It should produce accountable, responsive and legitimate government, though its real record is mixed. Democracy has no automatic growth advantage and often struggles with inequality. Its strengths are peaceful accommodation of diversity, equal political status, dignity, freedom, correction and public accountability. Citizens deepen these outcomes by participating, demanding information and judging power-holders.

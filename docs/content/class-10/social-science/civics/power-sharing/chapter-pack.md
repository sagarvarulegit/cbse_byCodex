# Chapter 1: Power-sharing

## Hook

Imagine that one group in your class always chooses the monitor, makes every rule and never listens to anyone else. Even if that group has more students, will the class remain united? Belgium and Sri Lanka show two very different answers to this question.

**Chapter goal:** Understand why democracy shares power, compare the different paths taken by Belgium and Sri Lanka, and recognise the main forms of power-sharing used in modern democracies.

## Overview

Democracy does not place all power in one person or one organ. The legislature, executive and judiciary share power. This chapter extends that idea through the stories of Belgium and Sri Lanka, draws lessons about why power-sharing is needed, and explains its common forms.

## Belgium and Sri Lanka

### Belgium: a small country with a complex population

- Belgium is smaller in area than Haryana and has a population of a little over one crore.
- **59 per cent** of its people live in the Flemish region and speak Dutch.
- **40 per cent** live in the Wallonia region and speak French.
- The remaining **1 per cent** speak German.
- In Brussels, the pattern is reversed: **80 per cent** speak French and **20 per cent** speak Dutch.
- The French-speaking community was relatively rich and powerful. The Dutch-speaking community received the benefits of development and education later. This created tension during the 1950s and 1960s, especially in Brussels.

### Sri Lanka: another diverse society

- Sri Lanka is an island nation a few kilometres from the southern coast of Tamil Nadu. Its population is about two crore.
- **74 per cent** of the people are Sinhala-speakers and **18 per cent** are Tamil-speakers.
- **13 per cent** are Sri Lankan Tamils. The other Tamils are called Indian Tamils because their forefathers came from India as plantation workers during colonial rule.
- Sri Lankan Tamils are concentrated in the north and east.
- Most Sinhala-speakers are Buddhists. Most Tamils are Hindus or Muslims. About **7 per cent** are Christians from both Sinhala and Tamil communities.

**Remember:** A numerical majority can use its numbers to dominate, or it can share power and protect unity. The two countries chose different paths.

## Majoritarianism in Sri Lanka

Sri Lanka became independent in **1948**. Sinhala leaders used their majority to establish Sinhala supremacy:

- In **1956**, an Act made Sinhala the only official language and ignored Tamil.
- Government policies favoured Sinhala applicants for university positions and government jobs.
- A new constitution said that the state would protect and foster Buddhism.

Sri Lankan Tamils felt that these policies denied equal political rights, discriminated against them in education and jobs, and ignored their language and culture. They demanded Tamil as an official language, regional autonomy, and equal opportunities. Repeated denial led some organisations in the 1980s to demand an independent Tamil Eelam in the north and east.

The conflict became a civil war. Thousands were killed, many became refugees and many lost their livelihoods. The war badly damaged the country’s social, cultural and economic life. It ended in **2009**.

**Key idea — Majoritarianism:** The belief that the majority community should be able to rule in any way it wants, without considering the wishes and needs of minorities.

### Textbook source guide

- **Glossary box — “Majoritarianism”:** The box defines rule based on the wishes of the majority while disregarding the minority. Sri Lanka’s language, job and religious policies show this danger.
- **Unnumbered student cartoon prompt:** “What’s wrong if the majority community rules? If Sinhalas don’t rule in Sri Lanka, where else will they rule?” The answer is that democracy is not permission for a permanent majority community to deny equal rights to others.
- **Glossary box — “Civil war”:** A violent conflict between opposing groups within a country that becomes so intense that it appears like a war.
- **Unnumbered student cartoon prompt:** “What kind of a solution is this? I am glad our Constitution does not say which minister will come from which community.” The prompt prepares us to examine why Belgium accepted special sharing rules to prevent domination.

## Accommodation in Belgium

Belgian leaders recognised regional and cultural differences. Between **1970 and 1993**, they amended the Constitution four times and built these arrangements:

- The central government has an equal number of Dutch-speaking and French-speaking ministers. Some special laws need the support of a majority from each language group, so neither group can decide alone.
- Many powers of the central government were given to State Governments of the two regions. These State Governments are not subordinate to the central government.
- Brussels has a separate government with equal representation of both communities.
- A third type, the **community government**, is elected by Dutch-, French- and German-speaking people, wherever they live. It handles cultural, educational and language-related matters.

The system is complicated, but it helped Belgium avoid civic conflict and a possible division of the country. Brussels later became the headquarters of the European Union.

## Why power sharing is desirable?

There are two kinds of reasons:

- **Prudential reason:** Power-sharing reduces conflict among social groups and supports political stability. Majority domination may look useful in the short run, but it can damage national unity in the long run. Even the majority can suffer under the tyranny of the majority.
- **Moral reason:** Power-sharing is the spirit of democracy. People affected by power have a right to be consulted. Participation gives citizens a stake in the system and makes a government legitimate.

**Memory trick:** **P = Practical peace; M = Moral participation.** Prudential reasons focus on better results. Moral reasons value sharing itself.

## Khalil’s dilemma

Khalil lives in Beirut. His father is an Orthodox Christian and his mother a Sunni Muslim. Lebanon’s communities had fought a civil war, in which one of Khalil’s uncles was killed. After the war, leaders agreed that:

- the President would be a Maronite Catholic Christian;
- the Prime Minister would be a Sunni Muslim;
- the Deputy Prime Minister would be an Orthodox Christian; and
- the Speaker would be a Shi’a Muslim.

Christians agreed not to seek French protection, and Muslims agreed not to seek union with Syria. Khalil dislikes fixed community posts because he does not identify with either parent’s religion and cannot reach the top office. Older people defend the pact as a guarantee of peace.

**The dilemma:** Should Lebanon follow ordinary winner-based election rules, keep the old arrangement, or improve it in another way? A careful answer should balance equal opportunity for individuals with safeguards that prevent another community conflict.

### Textbook source guide

- **Special illustrated story — “Khalil’s dilemma”:** Vikram drives a motorbike while Vetal tells Khalil’s story and asks him to choose rules for Lebanon. The printed closing prompt is: “Can you help poor Vikram in answering Vetal?” The story shows that a power-sharing rule may protect peace but can also limit an individual who does not fit fixed community identities.

## Forms of power-sharing

Modern democracies commonly share power in four ways:

1. **Among organs of government:** The legislature, executive and judiciary exercise different powers at the same level. This is the **horizontal distribution of power**. Checks and balances prevent any organ from using unlimited power.
2. **Among levels of government:** Central or Union, State, and local governments exercise powers at higher and lower levels. This is the **vertical division of power** or federal division of power.
3. **Among social groups:** Religious and linguistic communities, socially weaker sections and women may receive representation. Belgium’s community government and India’s reserved constituencies are examples.
4. **Among political parties, pressure groups and movements:** Parties compete, alliances may form coalition governments, and interest groups influence decisions or join government committees.

### Textbook source guide

- **Cartoon — “Reigning the Reins”:** The printed caption says that new Russian laws in 2005 gave more powers to its president and asks about the relationship between democracy and concentration of power. Its point is that concentrating the reins of power weakens democratic checks.
- **Unnumbered student cartoon prompt:** “In my school, the class monitor changes every month. Is that what you call a power sharing arrangement?” Changing one holder over time is not the chapter’s main meaning of sharing; power-sharing creates arrangements in which power is distributed among institutions, levels, groups or organisations.
- **Activity — four examples of power-sharing:** The Bombay High Court directing the Maharashtra Government shows power shared among organs; Ontario settling a land claim with an Aboriginal community shows sharing with a social group; two Russian parties forming a coalition shows sharing among parties; Nigerian State finance ministers questioning the federal government shows sharing among levels. The activity asks: “Which of the four types of power sharing do these represent? Who is sharing power with whom?”

## Exercises

### 1. What are the different forms of power sharing in modern democracies? Give an example of each of these.

**Answer:** Power is shared (i) horizontally among the legislature, executive and judiciary, as in India’s checks and balances; (ii) vertically among Union, State and local governments, as in Indian federalism; (iii) among social groups, as in Belgium’s community government or reserved constituencies in India; and (iv) among parties, pressure groups and movements, as in a coalition government.

### 2. State one prudential reason and one moral reason for power sharing with an example from the Indian context.

**Answer:** A prudential reason is that sharing power reduces social conflict and supports stability; India’s federal sharing respects regional diversity. A moral reason is that people affected by decisions have a right to participate; Panchayati Raj gives local people a direct voice in decisions.

### 3. After reading this chapter, three students drew different conclusions. Which of these do you agree with and why? Give your reasons in about 50 words.

Thomman - Power sharing is necessary only in societies which have religious, linguistic or ethnic divisions.

Mathayi – Power sharing is suitable only for big countries that have regional divisions.

Ouseph – Every society needs some form of power sharing even if it is small or does not have social divisions.

**Answer:** I agree with Ouseph. Every democracy needs checks and balances, citizen participation and accountability, even when a country is small or has few visible social divisions. Sharing power prevents arbitrary rule and gives people a voice. Social diversity makes some arrangements more urgent, but it is not the only reason for sharing power.

### 4. The Mayor of Merchtem, a town near Brussels in Belgium, has defended a ban on speaking French in the town’s schools. He said that the ban would help all non-Dutch speakers integrate in this Flemish town. Do you think that this measure is in keeping with the spirit of Belgium’s power sharing arrangements? Give your reasons in about 50 words.

**Answer:** No. Belgium’s arrangements respect Dutch-, French- and German-speaking communities and prevent one community from imposing its will. A ban on French ignores linguistic choice and follows the spirit of majoritarianism, not accommodation. Integration should be achieved through respect and cooperation, not by suppressing a community’s language.

### 5. Read the following passage and pick out any one of the prudential reasons for power sharing offered in this.

> “We need to give more power to the panchayats to realise the dream of Mahatma Gandhi and the hopes of the makers of our Constitution. Panchayati Raj establishes true democracy. Thus, Panchayati Raj will strengthen the foundations of our democracy.”

**Answer:** One prudential reason is that giving power to Panchayats can reduce corruption and increase administrative efficiency because local people can monitor the planning and implementation of development schemes.

## Common mistakes

- Saying that every decision by a numerical majority is democratic. Democracy also protects minorities and requires consultation.
- Mixing up horizontal sharing among organs with vertical sharing among levels.
- Writing that Belgium’s State Governments are subordinate to its central government. They are not.
- Treating prudential and moral reasons as the same. Prudential is about useful results; moral is about the democratic right to participate.
- Forgetting that the Sri Lankan civil war ended in 2009.

## Board tips

- In a Belgium–Sri Lanka comparison, organise the answer as **social composition → government policy → result**.
- For “forms of power-sharing,” name the form, identify who shares power, and give one textbook example.
- When asked why sharing is desirable, write one prudential and one moral reason separately.
- Use “horizontal = same level” and “vertical = different levels” to make the distinction clear.

## One-minute revision

Belgium used accommodation; Sri Lanka used majoritarianism. Belgium protected different language communities through central, State, Brussels and community governments. Sri Lanka’s Sinhala-only and preferential policies caused Tamil alienation and civil war. Power-sharing is desirable for prudential peace and moral participation. It can be horizontal, vertical, social-group based, or shared among parties and organised interests.

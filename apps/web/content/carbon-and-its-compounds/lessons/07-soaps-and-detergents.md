# 4.5 Soaps and detergents

Soaps and detergents clean oily dirt by using molecules with a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail.

This topic becomes easy when you remember one problem: oily dirt does not dissolve in water. Soap acts like a bridge between oil and water.

## Learning goals

By the end of this lesson, you should be able to:
- describe the structure of a soap molecule
- explain micelle formation
- compare soap and detergent in hard water
- explain why soap does not clean well in hard water
- write a clear board-style answer on cleansing action

## Soap molecule

A soap molecule has two parts:
- hydrophilic head
- hydrophobic tail

Hydrophilic means water-loving.

Hydrophobic means water-hating or oil-loving.

The ionic head stays near water. The hydrocarbon tail goes into grease or oil.

Textbook language:
Soaps are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids. The ionic end interacts with water. The long hydrocarbon chain interacts with oil or grease.

So a soap molecule has two personalities:
- head: likes water
- tail: likes hydrocarbons and oily dirt

## Micelle formation

When soap is added to water with oily dirt:
1. soap tails move into the oil or grease
2. soap heads stay in water
3. many soap molecules arrange around the oil drop
4. this spherical arrangement is called a micelle
5. the oil drop can now be washed away with water

Memory line:
`Tail in oil, head in water: micelle lifts the dirt.`

Micelles form in water because soap has a water-loving ionic end and an oil-loving hydrocarbon tail.

In solvents such as ethanol, the same kind of micelle does not form in the same way because ethanol can interact differently with both parts of the soap molecule. The strong water-versus-oil separation that drives micelle formation is not the same.

## Why micelles stay suspended

The oily dirt is trapped inside the micelle. The ionic heads face outward into water. Because the outside has charged ends, the micelles repel one another and do not easily come together as a precipitate. They remain suspended as a colloid and can be rinsed away.

This is also why soap solution can look cloudy: the micelles are large enough to scatter light.

## Cleansing action of soap

Oil and water do not mix well.

Soap solves this problem by forming micelles. The hydrophobic tails attach to oil. The hydrophilic heads face water. When water is stirred or rubbed, the oil breaks into small droplets surrounded by soap molecules.

These droplets are washed away.

Full board-style answer:
Soap molecules have a hydrophobic hydrocarbon tail and a hydrophilic ionic head. In water, the tails enter the oily dirt while the heads remain in water. Many soap molecules surround the oil drop and form a micelle. The oily dirt is held in the centre of the micelle. On rubbing or shaking, the dirt breaks into small droplets and remains suspended in water, so it can be rinsed away.

Agitation is needed while washing because rubbing, beating, or machine motion helps break oily dirt into small droplets surrounded by soap molecules.

## Soap in hard water

Hard water contains calcium and magnesium ions.

Soap reacts with these ions to form an insoluble substance called scum.

Because of scum:
- less foam is formed
- cleaning becomes less effective
- more soap is wasted

[[demo:activity-4-11-soap-hard-water]]

Soap is basic in nature, so a soap solution turns red litmus blue and does not turn blue litmus red.

The white curdy precipitate seen with soap in hard water is scum. It forms because calcium and magnesium salts in hard water react with soap. This wastes soap and reduces foam. That is why more soap is needed in hard water.

## Detergents

Detergents are cleansing agents that work better in hard water.

They do not form insoluble scum as easily as soap.

This is why detergents are used in many washing powders and liquids.

The textbook describes detergents as generally sodium salts of sulphonic acids or ammonium salts with chlorides or bromides, etc., with long hydrocarbon chains. Their charged ends do not form insoluble precipitates with calcium and magnesium ions in hard water, so they remain effective.

[[demo:activity-4-12-soap-vs-detergent]]

You cannot check whether water is hard by using a detergent, because detergents lather even in hard water.

## Saponification idea

Soap can be prepared by heating oil or fat with sodium hydroxide.

This process is called saponification.

Products:
- soap
- glycerol

The textbook group activity uses castor oil, cotton seed oil, linseed oil, or soyabean oil with sodium hydroxide. Common salt is added later to help separate the soap. This connects back to ester chemistry: oils and fats are converted into soap and glycerol.

## Questions and answers

### 4.5 Soaps and detergents

Question:
How do soaps remove oily dirt?

Answer:
Soap molecules form micelles with their hydrophobic tails around oil and hydrophilic heads in water, so the dirt can be washed away.

Question:
Why do soaps not work well in hard water?

Answer:
Hard water contains calcium and magnesium ions that react with soap to form insoluble scum.

Question:
Why are detergents better in hard water?

Answer:
Detergents do not form insoluble scum easily with calcium and magnesium ions, so they clean better in hard water.

Do You Know:
- A soap molecule has a water-loving head and an oil-loving tail.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: Micelle

Question:
What is a micelle?

Answer:
A micelle is a spherical arrangement of soap molecules around an oil droplet, with tails inside the oil and heads facing water.

### Example 2: Hard water

Question:
Why does soap not work well in hard water?

Answer:
Soap reacts with calcium and magnesium ions in hard water to form insoluble scum.

### Example 3: Detergent

Question:
Why are detergents preferred in hard water?

Answer:
Detergents do not form insoluble scum easily, so they clean better in hard water.

### Example 4: Scum

Question:
Explain the formation of scum when hard water is treated with soap.

Answer:
Calcium and magnesium ions in hard water react with soap to form an insoluble curdy precipitate called scum.

### Example 5: Micelles in ethanol

Question:
Will soap form the same kind of micelle in ethanol as it does in water?

Answer:
No. Micelle formation depends on the hydrophilic head interacting with water and the hydrophobic tail avoiding water. Ethanol interacts differently, so the same water-based micelle arrangement is not formed.

### Example 6: Agitation

Question:
Why do we beat, scrub, or agitate clothes after adding soap?

Answer:
Agitation breaks oily dirt into small droplets and helps soap molecules surround them as micelles, so the dirt can be rinsed away.

## Common mistakes

1. Saying soap dissolves oil directly.
2. Mixing up hydrophilic and hydrophobic parts.
3. Forgetting calcium and magnesium ions in hard water.
4. Saying detergents and soaps behave exactly the same in hard water.

## Board tips

- In cleansing-action answers, draw or describe heads outside and tails inside.
- Use exact terms: hydrophilic, hydrophobic, micelle, scum.
- For hard water questions, mention calcium and magnesium ions.

## One-minute revision

Soap molecules are sodium or potassium salts of long-chain carboxylic acids. They have water-loving ionic heads and oil-loving hydrocarbon tails. They form micelles around oily dirt, keeping dirt suspended so it can be washed away. Soap works poorly in hard water because it forms scum with calcium and magnesium ions. Detergents clean better in hard water because they do not form such insoluble precipitates easily.

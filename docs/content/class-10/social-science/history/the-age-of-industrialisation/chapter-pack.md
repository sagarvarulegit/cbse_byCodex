# The Age of Industrialisation

## Hook

Did the factory suddenly replace every handloom and workshop? The textbook's answer is no. Machines, hand labour, homes, workshops and factories existed together, and industrial change affected workers, merchants and consumers in very different ways.

## Chapter goal

Understand why industrialisation was gradual, how colonial rule changed Indian textiles and factories, why small producers survived, and how advertising created markets for new goods.

## Opening context (unnumbered in the textbook)

Images such as *Dawn of the Century* and *Two Magicians* celebrated machines as progress and the West as modern. The chapter tests this triumphant story by asking what industrialisation actually meant for production and people's lives in Britain and colonial India.

## 1 Before the Industrial Revolution

Before factories spread, merchants supplied money and raw material to rural households that produced goods for international markets. This is called proto-industrialisation. Town guilds restricted new producers, while poor peasants welcomed extra income. Merchants controlled a wide network, but families worked from their homes.

### 1.1 The Coming Up of the Factory

Cotton led early industrial growth. New inventions improved carding, twisting, spinning and rolling. Richard Arkwright's cotton mill brought expensive machines and many stages of production under one roof, allowing closer supervision, quality control and regulation of labour.

### 1.2 The Pace of Industrial Change

Industrial change was not instant. Cotton led first, followed by iron and steel as railways expanded. Most workers remained outside advanced factory sectors, traditional industries continued, and small innovations improved ordinary trades. Steam power spread slowly because machines were costly, unreliable and expensive to repair.

## 2 Hand Labour and Steam Power

Britain had abundant, low-paid workers, so many industrialists did not want costly labour-saving machines. Seasonal industries needed workers only at busy times. Hand labour also made varied, carefully designed goods for wealthy customers, while machines were better at uniform goods for mass markets.

### 2.1 Life of the Workers

Workers travelled to cities but jobs depended on friends, relatives and seasonal demand. Many waited for weeks, slept in shelters or on streets and suffered long periods without work. Fear of job loss made workers hostile to machines such as the Spinning Jenny. After the 1840s, city construction and railways created more jobs.

## 3 Industrialisation in the Colonies

India's experience was shaped by colonial rule. The chapter follows textiles because hand production, Company control, imports and new factories all met in this sector.

### 3.1 The Age of Indian Textiles

Before machine industry, Indian silk and fine cotton dominated international markets. Indian merchants, bankers, supply merchants and brokers financed and moved cloth through Surat, Hooghly and Masulipatam. European companies gained monopoly rights, old ports declined, and Bombay and Calcutta grew under colonial control.

### 3.2 What Happened to Weavers?

The East India Company appointed paid *gomasthas* to supervise weavers, gave advances and stopped them selling to other buyers. Low prices, punishment and debt caused conflict, migration and revolt. The weavers' bargaining power disappeared even while Company exports initially grew.

### 3.3 Manchester Comes to India

British tariffs protected Manchester cloth, while the Company opened India to British imports. Indian weavers lost export and home markets because cheap machine-made cloth flooded India. During the American Civil War, scarce raw cotton became expensive; later Indian factory goods created further competition.

## 4 Factories Come Up

Bombay's first cotton mill began production in 1856; jute mills grew in Bengal, and mills appeared in Kanpur, Ahmedabad and Madras. New questions arose: who supplied capital, who owned the mills and where did workers come from?

### 4.1 The Early Entrepreneurs

Indian industrial capital came from trade networks, including the China trade, raw-cotton exports and internal banking. Dwarkanath Tagore, Dinshaw Petit, Jamsetjee Tata, Seth Hukumchand and the Birla family were among early business figures. Yet European managing agencies controlled major sectors and limited Indian business opportunities.

### 4.2 Where Did the Workers Come From?

Most workers came from nearby villages, though some travelled long distances. They often moved between city mills and village harvests. A *jobber* recruited workers, helped them settle and lent money, but could demand gifts and exercise control. Factory work remained only one part of India's industrial workforce.

## 5 The Peculiarities of Industrial Growth

European agencies invested mainly in export products such as tea, coffee, indigo, jute and mining. Indian mills first produced yarn rather than compete directly with Manchester cloth. Swadeshi, tariff pressure and the First World War expanded Indian production as British imports fell and war demand rose.

### 5.1 Small-scale Industries Predominate

Most industrial workers remained in small workshops and households. Handloom output grew because weavers adopted tools such as the fly shuttle and because mills could not copy specialised designs. Fine cloth had stable wealthy buyers, but coarse-cloth producers suffered badly during famines. Survival did not mean prosperity: whole families often worked long hours.

## 6 Market for Goods

Producers used labels, advertisements and calendars to create trust and desire. “Made in Manchester” became a mark of quality. Images of Indian gods, rulers and familiar scenes made foreign goods seem respectable and local. Indian manufacturers later used swadeshi messages to connect buying Indian goods with service to the nation.

## Conclusion

Industrialisation brought factories, technology and a new labour force, but hand production and small-scale industry remained central. Looking again at the opening images, we can now see that “progress” hid slow change, hard work, colonial inequality and the survival of many older forms of production.

## Textbook end exercises with answer support

### Write in brief

1. **“Explain the following:”**

   a) **“Women workers in Britain attacked the Spinning Jenny.”** It reduced the labour needed for spinning, so women feared loss of work and income.

   b) **“In the seventeenth century merchants from towns in Europe began employing peasants and artisans within the villages.”** Town guilds controlled production and blocked new merchants. Rural families needed extra income, so merchants supplied advances and organised home production.

   c) **“The port of Surat declined by the end of the eighteenth century.”** European companies gained trade monopoly, colonial ports Bombay and Calcutta grew, old credit networks weakened and Surat's trade value fell sharply.

   d) **“The East India Company appointed gomasthas to supervise weavers in India.”** The Company wanted direct control, regular supply, lower costs and no competition from other buyers. Gomasthas supervised, collected and checked cloth.

2. **“Write True or False against each statement:”**

   a) **“At the end of the nineteenth century, 80 per cent of the total workforce in Europe was employed in the technologically advanced industrial sector.” — False.** Less than 20 per cent worked in technologically advanced sectors.

   b) **“The international market for fine textiles was dominated by India till the eighteenth century.” — True.**

   c) **“The American Civil War resulted in the reduction of cotton exports from India.” — False.** With US supplies cut off, Britain's demand for Indian raw cotton increased.

   d) **“The introduction of the fly shuttle enabled handloom workers to improve their productivity.” — True.**

3. **“Explain what is meant by proto-industrialisation.”**

   **Answer:** It was large-scale production for international markets before factory industrialisation. Town merchants supplied rural peasants and artisans with money and materials; households produced goods in villages, and merchants controlled the network.

### Discuss

1. **“Why did some industrialists in nineteenth-century Europe prefer hand labour over machines?”**

   **Answer:** Labour was plentiful and cheap; many industries were seasonal; machines needed costly investment and repair; and varied, intricate or luxury goods required flexible human skill.

2. **“How did the East India Company procure regular supplies of cotton and silk textiles from Indian weavers?”**

   **Answer:** It removed competing traders, appointed gomasthas, gave weavers advances for raw material, required borrowers to sell only to the Company, inspected cloth and used sepoys and punishment to enforce control.

3. **“Imagine that you have been asked to write an article for an encyclopaedia on Britain and the history of cotton. Write your piece using information from the entire chapter.”**

   **Answer frame:** Cover proto-industry, cotton inventions, Arkwright's mill, slow factory growth, hand labour, imports of Indian textiles, tariffs protecting Manchester, sale of British cloth in India, and wartime decline of Manchester exports.

4. **“Why did industrial production in India increase during the First World War?”**

   **Answer:** British mills concentrated on war needs, so Manchester imports to India fell. Indian mills gained a large home market and supplied jute bags, uniforms, tents, leather goods and other war materials. Old factories ran several shifts and new workers and factories were added.

**“Select any one industry in your region and find out its history. How has the technology changed? Where do the workers come from? How are the products advertised and marketed? Try and talk to the employers and some workers to get their views about the industry's history.”**

Prepare a dated source list, ask the same core questions to employer and worker, and separate verified facts from personal memories.

## Common mistakes students make

- Equating industrialisation only with factories.
- Assuming machines spread quickly and replaced all hand production.
- Confusing a seventeenth-century “factory” trading post with a modern mill.
- Saying Manchester imports helped Indian weavers; they damaged both export and home markets.
- Treating the survival of handloom workers as proof that they were prosperous.
- Forgetting that advertisements and labels helped create demand.

## Board-answer tips

- Compare Britain and India by organising answers under labour, technology, market and state power.
- For “why hand labour?”, give at least three distinct reasons, not three versions of “labour was cheap”.
- In Company-weaver answers, write the sequence: eliminate traders → appoint gomastha → advances → exclusive supply → coercion.
- Use evidence such as “less than 20 per cent” or the fly shuttle only when it directly supports the point.
- Analyse an image by naming what it shows, the message it gives and what it leaves out.

### Answer check

1. Market production organised by merchants through rural households before factories.
2. To supervise labour, quality and the connected stages of machine production.
3. Any two: high price, repair cost, breakdowns, weak performance, plentiful cheap labour.
4. It reduced demand for hand spinners and threatened income.
5. A borrower had to supply the Company and could not sell to a better-paying buyer.
6. British imports fell and Indian mills received large war orders.
7. It raised output, reduced labour time and allowed wider cloth.
8. Any two: familiar gods, respected rulers, calendars, quality labels, swadeshi messages.

## One-minute revision

- Proto-industry joined town merchants with rural household labour.
- Factories grew gradually; hand and machine work existed together.
- Cheap, plentiful and seasonal labour limited mechanisation in Britain.
- Company gomasthas controlled Indian weavers through advances and coercion.
- Manchester cloth damaged Indian export and home markets.
- Trade capital and internal banking helped Indian entrepreneurs start mills.
- Most Indian industrial workers remained in small units; the fly shuttle improved handloom output.
- Labels, gods, rulers and swadeshi messages created markets.

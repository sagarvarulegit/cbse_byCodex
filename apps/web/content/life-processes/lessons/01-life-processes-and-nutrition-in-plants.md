# 5.1 What are life processes? and 5.2 Nutrition

Life is maintained by invisible molecular work. A living body must repair, organise, move materials, release energy, and remove wastes even when the organism is resting.

The textbook begins with a simple warning: visible movement alone is not enough to decide whether something is alive. Plants may not visibly move every minute, and a sleeping animal may look still, but life continues through molecular movements.

## 5.1 What are life processes?

Living organisms are organised structures. Cells, tissues, organs, and organ systems can be damaged by the environment over time, so the body must keep repairing and maintaining itself.

The main maintenance processes are:
- nutrition: taking in food or raw materials for energy and growth
- respiration: breaking food down to release usable energy
- transportation: moving materials from one part to another
- excretion: removing harmful metabolic wastes

In unicellular organisms, the whole surface is in contact with the environment. But in multicellular organisms such as humans, all cells are not directly exposed to air, food, or waste-removal surfaces. Simple diffusion cannot meet every cell's needs quickly enough.

Memory line:
`Big body = specialised tissues + transport system.`

## Questions and answers

### 5.1 What are life processes?

Question:
Why is diffusion insufficient to meet the oxygen requirements of multi-cellular organisms like humans?

Answer:
In multi-cellular organisms like humans, all cells are not in direct contact with the surrounding environment. Oxygen is taken in at specialised surfaces, so simple diffusion would be too slow to supply oxygen to every cell of the body.

Question:
What criteria do we use to decide whether something is alive?

Answer:
We use movement as an important sign of life, including visible movement like growth, breathing, or body movement, and invisible molecular movement inside cells. Living organisms also maintain their organised structures through life processes.

Question:
What are outside raw materials used for by an organism?

Answer:
Outside raw materials are used to obtain energy and to build and repair the body. Food provides energy and carbon-based materials, oxygen helps release energy from food, and other materials support growth and maintenance.

Question:
What processes would you consider essential for maintaining life?

Answer:
The essential life processes are nutrition, respiration, transportation, and excretion. Together, they supply energy and materials, move substances through the body, and remove harmful wastes.

## Why food is needed

Food provides:
- energy for molecular movement and maintenance
- raw material for growth
- carbon-based molecules used to build body structures

Different organisms use different nutrition methods because food sources differ. Some can use simple inorganic materials; others need complex food made by other organisms.

## 5.2 Nutrition

Nutrition is the process by which an organism obtains food or raw materials for energy, growth, repair, and maintenance.

The textbook first names the parent topic as `5.2 Nutrition`, then explains the major modes of nutrition. This lesson begins that parent topic with autotrophic nutrition in plants.

## 5.2.1 Autotrophic nutrition

Autotrophs make their own food from simple inorganic substances.

Green plants and some bacteria are autotrophs.

In plants, photosynthesis converts carbon dioxide and water into carbohydrates in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll.

Equation:

`6CO2 + 12H2O --sunlight/chlorophyll--> C6H12O6 + 6O2 + 6H2O`

After cancelling the six water molecules formed on the right, this is often
written in the net form `6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2`. Both are balanced, but
the first form makes the board-marking sequence of water splitting and water
formation explicit. The important textbook idea is that light energy is
absorbed, water is split, and carbon dioxide is reduced to carbohydrates.

## Events in photosynthesis

During photosynthesis:

1. chlorophyll absorbs light energy
2. light energy changes into chemical energy
3. water molecules split into hydrogen and oxygen
4. carbon dioxide is reduced to carbohydrates

The carbohydrate not used immediately is stored as starch in plants. In our body, extra energy from food can be stored as glycogen.

Desert plants may take carbon dioxide at night and use absorbed light energy during the day. So the steps do not always need to happen immediately one after another.

Activity 5.1: Variegated leaf starch test
- Keep a variegated plant in darkness so stored starch is used.
- Keep it in sunlight, then test a leaf with iodine after removing chlorophyll using alcohol in a water bath.
- Observation focus: green areas show starch more clearly.
- Conclusion: chlorophyll is essential for photosynthesis.

Activity 5.2: Carbon dioxide is essential for photosynthesis
- Destarch two similar healthy potted plants by keeping them in darkness for three days.
- Place each plant on a separate glass plate. Keep potassium hydroxide in a watch-glass beside one plant; it absorbs carbon dioxide.
- Cover each plant with an airtight bell jar, seal the base with vaseline, and keep both in sunlight for about two hours.
- Test one leaf from each plant for starch.
- Observation: the leaf in the jar without potassium hydroxide shows more starch.
- Conclusion: carbon dioxide is essential for photosynthesis.

Textbook design prompt:
Using Activities 5.1 and 5.2 as models, cover part of a destarched leaf with opaque paper, expose the plant to sunlight, and perform the starch test. Only the exposed part turns blue-black, showing that sunlight is essential for photosynthesis.

Board safety note:
Alcohol is heated in a water bath, not directly over a flame, because alcohol is inflammable.

## How plants get carbon dioxide

Stomata are tiny pores on the surface of leaves. Large amounts of gas exchange happen through stomata during photosynthesis.

But plants can lose water through stomata. So guard cells control opening and closing:
- guard cells swell with water: pore opens
- guard cells shrink: pore closes

Gas exchange can also occur across stems and roots, but leaves are the main photosynthesis surface.

## Carbon dioxide and other raw materials

Carbon dioxide enters mainly through stomata.

Water is absorbed from soil by roots.

Plants also need minerals such as nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and magnesium. Nitrogen is needed for proteins and other compounds. It may be taken as nitrates, nitrites, or organic compounds prepared by bacteria from atmospheric nitrogen.

## Solved examples

### Example 1: Diffusion

Question:
Why is diffusion insufficient in multicellular organisms like humans?

Answer:
Multicellular organisms have many cells deep inside the body. These cells are not in direct contact with the environment, so diffusion alone is too slow to supply oxygen and food or remove wastes.

### Example 2: Life processes

Question:
What processes are essential for maintaining life?

Answer:
Nutrition, respiration, transportation, and excretion are essential life processes.

### Example 3: Photosynthesis requirements

Question:
What conditions are necessary for autotrophic nutrition?

Answer:
Autotrophic nutrition in green plants needs carbon dioxide, water, sunlight, and chlorophyll. Oxygen is released as a by-product.

### Example 4: Guard cells

Question:
How do guard cells control stomata?

Answer:
Guard cells swell when water enters them and open the pore. They shrink when water leaves and close the pore.

## Common mistakes

1. Saying visible movement is the only sign of life.
2. Writing that diffusion is enough for humans because oxygen can diffuse.
3. Forgetting carbon dioxide and water in photosynthesis conditions.
4. Saying chlorophyll is food instead of the pigment that absorbs light.
5. Heating alcohol directly during the leaf-starch activity.

## One-minute revision

Life processes maintain organised living structures. Multicellular organisms need specialised systems because diffusion is too slow for all cells. Plants use autotrophic nutrition. Photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide, water, sunlight, and chlorophyll, and it stores food as carbohydrate or starch. Stomata allow gas exchange, while guard cells control water loss by opening and closing the pore.

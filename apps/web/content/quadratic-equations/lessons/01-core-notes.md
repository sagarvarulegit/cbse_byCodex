# 4.1-4.5 Quadratic Equations

A rectangular hall, two consecutive integers, and a circular-park pole can all create the same kind of equation: one whose highest power is $2$.

## 4.1 Introduction

The prayer-hall problem in Figure 4.1 has area $300\text{ m}^2$, length $2x+1$ and breadth $x$. It gives

$$x(2x+1)=300\quad\Rightarrow\quad2x^2+x-300=0.$$

## 4.2 Quadratic Equations

A quadratic equation in $x$ has standard form

$$ax^2+bx+c=0,\qquad a\ne0.$$

Always simplify an equation before deciding its degree. An equation that first looks cubic may reduce to a quadratic after like terms cancel.

**Examples 1-2** translate situations into equations and distinguish quadratic equations from non-quadratic ones.

## 4.3 Solution of a Quadratic Equation by Factorisation

A root is a value of $x$ that makes the equation true. To solve by factorisation:

1. write the equation in standard form
2. split the middle term or identify factors
3. use the zero-product rule
4. check whether the roots suit the situation

**Example 3**

$$2x^2-5x+3=(2x-3)(x-1)=0.$$

The roots are $3/2$ and $1$.

**Example 4**

$$6x^2-x-2=(3x-2)(2x+1)=0.$$

The roots are $2/3$ and $-1/2$.

**Example 5** has the equal irrational root $\sqrt{2/3}$ twice.

**Example 6** returns to the prayer hall. The roots are $12$ and $-12.5$, but a breadth cannot be negative, so the hall is $12\text{ m}$ by $25\text{ m}$.

## 4.4 Nature of Roots

For $ax^2+bx+c=0$, the quadratic formula is

$$x=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}.$$

The discriminant is

$$D=b^2-4ac.$$

| Discriminant | Nature of real roots |
| --- | --- |
| $D>0$ | two distinct real roots |
| $D=0$ | two equal real roots, each $-b/(2a)$ |
| $D<0$ | no real roots |

**Example 7** has $D=-8$, so $2x^2-4x+3=0$ has no real roots.

**Example 8** uses the circular-park model in Figure 4.2. Only the positive distance $x=5$ m is physically admissible.

**Example 9** has $D=0$ and equal roots $1/3,1/3$.

## 4.5 Summary

- Standard form is $ax^2+bx+c=0$, $a\ne0$.
- Factorisation and the quadratic formula give real roots.
- The discriminant tells the number and type of real roots.
- Context decides whether a mathematical root is usable.

## Common mistakes

- identifying $a$, $b$, $c$ before moving everything to one side
- using $b$ without its sign
- writing $\sqrt{b^2}-4ac$ instead of $\sqrt{b^2-4ac}$
- keeping a negative length, age or count

## Board tips

- State the discriminant before discussing the roots.
- In a word problem, reject an inadmissible root with a reason.
- Verify factorisation by multiplying the factors once.

## Quick practice

1. Solve $x^2-5x+6=0$.
2. Find the nature of roots of $3x^2-4x+2=0$.
3. Find $k$ if $2x^2+kx+8=0$ has equal roots.

## Answer check

1. $x=2,3$.
2. $D=16-24=-8$; no real roots.
3. $k^2-64=0$, so $k=\pm8$.

## One-minute revision

Standard form first. Then factor or use the formula. Use $D$ to read the roots.

## Comic recap

- Standard form: "Bring every term to my side."
- Factorisation: "Split me into two zero-producing factors."
- Discriminant: "My sign predicts the real roots."
- Context: "Not every algebraic root fits real life."

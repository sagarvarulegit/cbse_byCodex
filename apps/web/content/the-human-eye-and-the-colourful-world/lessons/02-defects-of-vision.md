# 10.2 Defects of Vision and Their Correction

| Defect | Can see | Uncorrected image | Cause | Correction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| myopia (near-sightedness) | near clearly, distant blurred | in front of retina | excessive lens curvature or elongated eyeball | concave/diverging lens |
| hypermetropia (far-sightedness) | distant clearly, near blurred | behind retina | eye-lens focal length too long or eyeball too short | convex/converging lens |
| presbyopia | near point recedes with age | near focus difficult | weakened ciliary muscles and reduced lens flexibility | convex reading lens |

<ActivityIllustration demoId="figure-10-2-10-3-vision-defects" />

Some people have myopia and presbyopia together. Bifocal lenses may then use an
upper concave part for distant vision and a lower convex part for near vision.
Modern correction may use suitable contact lenses or surgery as advised.

## Correction calculation patterns

### Myopia far point 80 cm

For a distant object `u=-∞`, correction must make a virtual image at `v=-0.80m`.
Thus `f=-0.80m`, `P=1/f=-1.25D`: concave lens.

### Hypermetropic near point 1 m

The lens must make an object at `u=-0.25m` appear at `v=-1.0m`.

`1/f = 1/v - 1/u = -1 - (-4) = +3`

So `P=+3D`, `f=+0.333m`: convex lens.

## In-text questions

1. Power of accommodation: ability to adjust eye-lens focal length.
2. Myopic far point 1.2 m: use a concave lens.
3. Normal far point infinity; normal near point about 25 cm.
4. Difficulty seeing the distant blackboard: myopia; correct with suitable
   concave lens.

## Diagram answer method

Show the retina and eye lens; show where rays would focus without correction;
place the proper spectacle lens; show corrected rays focused on retina. Never
reverse concave and convex corrections.

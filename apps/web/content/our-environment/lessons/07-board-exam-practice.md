# Board Exam Practice

## Recurring demand families

1. Define ecosystem and classify biotic/abiotic items.
2. Distinguish natural and artificial ecosystems.
3. Classify producer, consumer types, decomposer.
4. Explain decomposer role without saying energy is recycled.
5. Build a valid food chain with arrows food→consumer.
6. Identify and number trophic levels.
7. Convert linked chains into a food web and explain stability.
8. Apply 10% law to energy calculations.
9. Explain why food chains are short and top populations small.
10. State unidirectional energy flow versus cyclic materials.
11. Predict consequences of trophic-level removal.
12. Define biomagnification and locate maximum concentration.
13. Distinguish energy decrease from pollutant increase.
14. Explain ozone formation and UV protection.
15. Link CFCs, ozone depletion, UV, and biological harm.
16. Distinguish ozone depletion from global warming.
17. Classify biodegradable/non-biodegradable with reason.
18. Give two impacts of each waste type.
19. Explain why biodegradable waste can still pollute.
20. Apply reduce, reuse, segregation, composting, recycling.
21. Evaluate plastic/paper/clay/reusable choices by life cycle.
22. Give safe e-waste/sewage/industrial-waste actions.

## 10% numerical frame

Write trophic order, count transfers, multiply by `0.10` once per transfer.
If a tertiary consumer has 50 J, secondary had 500 J, primary 5,000 J,
producer 50,000 J.

## Case-study frame

Identify ecosystem/component -> trace food/energy arrows -> apply 10% or
biomagnification -> predict disturbance -> propose a specific preventive action.

## High-value traps

- Food-chain arrows do not point toward the food.
- Producers are first trophic level; herbivores second.
- Only about 10% transfers; not 90%.
- Energy is not recycled; nutrients/materials are.
- Top consumers have maximum persistent-pollutant concentration.
- “Biodegradable” does not mean harmless in unlimited amounts.

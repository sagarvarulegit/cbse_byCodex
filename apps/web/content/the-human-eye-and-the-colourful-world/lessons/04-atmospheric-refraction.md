# 10.5 Atmospheric Refraction

Air layers at different temperatures and densities have different refractive
indices. Their changing conditions continuously bend light paths.

<ActivityIllustration demoId="figure-10-9-10-10-atmospheric-refraction" />

## Twinkling of stars

1. A star is extremely distant and acts as a point source.
2. Its light passes through continuously changing atmospheric layers.
3. Refraction changes apparent position and the amount of light entering the eye.
4. The star appears slightly higher and fluctuates between brighter and fainter:
   twinkling.

## Why planets do not twinkle

A nearby planet appears as an extended source—many point sources. Fluctuations
from different points average out, so total brightness is nearly steady.

## Advanced sunrise and delayed sunset

Atmospheric refraction bends sunlight so we see the Sun when it is slightly
below the horizon:

- sunrise appears about 2 minutes early;
- sunset appears about 2 minutes late;
- apparent day is about 4 minutes longer.

The same refraction causes apparent flattening of the Sun near the horizon.

## Hot-air wavering

Air above a fire has varying temperature and refractive index. An object's
apparent position fluctuates, producing wavering—atmospheric refraction on a
small scale.

## Full-mark comparison

Star: point source + varying refraction -> noticeable intensity fluctuation.
Planet: extended source + averaging -> no visible twinkle.

# 3.1-3.4 Pair of Linear Equations in Two Variables

Two real-life conditions can point to one answer, no answer, or many answers. On a graph, those three cases become intersecting, parallel or coincident lines.

## 3.1 Introduction

A linear equation in two variables can be written as $ax+by+c=0$, where $a$ and $b$ are not both zero. A pair asks for values of $x$ and $y$ that satisfy both equations at the same time.

The opening fair example uses ride count $x$ and hoopla attempts $y$:

$$y=\frac12x,\qquad 3x+4y=20.$$

## 3.2 Graphical Method of Solution of a Pair of Linear Equations

Each equation forms a line.

| Lines | Coefficient condition | Number of solutions | Name |
| --- | --- | ---: | --- |
| intersect once | $a_1/a_2\ne b_1/b_2$ | one | consistent, independent |
| coincide | $a_1/a_2=b_1/b_2=c_1/c_2$ | infinitely many | consistent, dependent |
| are parallel | $a_1/a_2=b_1/b_2\ne c_1/c_2$ | none | inconsistent |

**Example 1**

The lines $x+3y=6$ and $2x-3y=12$ intersect at $(6,0)$. Therefore the pair has the unique solution $x=6$, $y=0$.

**Example 2**

The pair $5x-8y+1=0$ and $3x-\frac{24}{5}y+\frac35=0$ represents coincident lines, so it has infinitely many solutions.

**Example 3**

Champa buys pants and skirts. Translating both statements gives $y=2x-2$ and $y=4x-4$. The lines meet at $(1,0)$, so she bought one pant and no skirt.

## 3.3 Algebraic Methods of Solving a Pair of Linear Equations

### 3.3.1 Substitution Method

1. Express one variable in terms of the other.
2. Substitute in the second equation.
3. Solve the one-variable equation.
4. Substitute back and verify both original equations.

**Example 4**

For $7x-15y=2$ and $x+2y=3$, substitution gives

$$x=\frac{49}{29},\qquad y=\frac{19}{29}.$$

Examples 5-7 show that substitution may lead to one value, an identity such as $0=0$, or a false statement such as $8=12$. These correspond to one, infinitely many, or no solution.

### 3.3.2 Elimination Method

1. Multiply one or both equations so one variable has equal coefficients.
2. Add or subtract the equations to eliminate that variable.
3. Solve for the remaining variable.
4. Substitute back and verify.

**Example 8**

Income ratio $9:7$, expenditure ratio $4:3$, and equal savings of Rs 2000 lead to monthly incomes of Rs 18000 and Rs 14000.

**Example 9**

Elimination applied to $2x+3y=8$ and $4x+6y=7$ produces $0=9$. The pair has no solution.

**Example 10**

A two-digit number problem gives $x+y=6$ and $x-y=2$. The solutions $(4,2)$ and $(2,4)$ produce the two possible numbers $42$ and $24$.

## 3.4 Summary

- A common ordered pair solves both equations.
- Graphs and coefficient ratios describe the same three cases.
- Substitution and elimination are the prescribed algebraic methods.
- Word problems must first be translated into two equations.

## Common mistakes

- comparing coefficient ratios before writing both equations in standard form
- changing a sign incorrectly while moving a term
- stopping after finding only one variable
- accepting $0=0$ as one particular solution instead of infinitely many

## Board tips

- Define both variables with units before forming equations.
- Verify a unique answer in both original equations.
- State the interpretation: unique, no solution, or infinitely many solutions.

## Quick practice

1. Classify $2x+3y=7$ and $4x+6y=14$.
2. Solve $x+y=5$ and $x-y=1$.
3. Classify $x+2y=3$ and $2x+4y=9$.

## Answer check

1. Coincident; infinitely many solutions.
2. $x=3$, $y=2$.
3. Parallel; no solution.

## One-minute revision

Different slopes meet once. Same line gives many answers. Same slope, different line gives none.

## Comic recap

- Intersecting lines: "We share exactly one point."
- Coincident lines: "Every point is shared."
- Parallel lines: "We never meet."
- Algebra: "Substitute or eliminate, then verify."

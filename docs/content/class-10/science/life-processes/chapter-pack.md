## 5.1 What are life processes?

Living organisms must maintain their organised body structures. Even when an organism is resting, molecular movements continue for repair, growth, energy use, and waste removal.

Essential life processes:
- nutrition: taking in food or raw materials
- respiration: breaking food to release energy
- transportation: moving materials through the body
- excretion: removing harmful metabolic wastes

Diffusion alone is not enough in large multicellular organisms because all cells are not directly exposed to the environment.

## 5.2 Nutrition

Nutrition is the process by which organisms obtain food or raw materials for energy, growth, and repair.

### 5.2.1 Autotrophic nutrition

Autotrophs such as green plants make food from carbon dioxide and water using sunlight and chlorophyll.

Photosynthesis summary:

`6CO2 + 6H2O -> C6H12O6 + 6O2`

Important textbook steps:
- chlorophyll absorbs light energy
- water splits into hydrogen and oxygen
- carbon dioxide is reduced to carbohydrates
- unused carbohydrate may be stored as starch

Activity and figure anchors:
- Activity 5.1: variegated leaf starch test proves chlorophyll is essential
- Activity 5.2: design a test to prove sunlight is essential
- Figure 5.1: cross-section of a leaf
- Figure 5.2: variegated leaf before and after starch test
- Figure 5.3: open and closed stomatal pore
- Figure 5.4: bell-jar setup with and without potassium hydroxide

Stomata allow gas exchange. Guard cells open the pore when swollen and close it when they shrink.

Plants also need minerals such as nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and magnesium from soil. Nitrogen helps make proteins and other compounds.

### 5.2.2 Heterotrophic nutrition

Heterotrophs depend directly or indirectly on autotrophs for food. Their food is complex and must be broken into simpler substances by enzymes.

Types:
- saprophytic: fungi such as bread moulds, yeast, and mushrooms break food outside the body and absorb it
- holozoic: animals take food in and digest it internally
- parasitic: organisms such as Cuscuta, ticks, lice, leeches, and tapeworms take nutrition from hosts

### 5.2.3 How do organisms obtain their nutrition?

Figure 5.5 shows nutrition in Amoeba. Amoeba uses pseudopodia to surround food and form a food vacuole. Paramoecium uses cilia to move food to a specific spot.

### 5.2.4 Nutrition in human beings

Food path:

`mouth -> oesophagus -> stomach -> small intestine -> large intestine -> anus`

Figure 5.6 shows the human alimentary canal.

Key roles:
- saliva contains salivary amylase and starts starch digestion
- peristalsis pushes food through the gut
- stomach glands release hydrochloric acid, pepsin, and mucus
- bile makes the medium alkaline and emulsifies fats
- pancreatic juice digests carbohydrates, proteins, and fats
- small intestine completes digestion and absorbs food through villi
- large intestine absorbs water

Activity 5.3 tests the action of saliva on starch using iodine.

Remember dental caries: bacteria act on food left in the mouth and produce acids that damage enamel.

## 5.3 Respiration

Respiration releases energy from food in a usable form called ATP.

Glucose first breaks into pyruvate in the cytoplasm. Then pyruvate follows different pathways:
- with oxygen in mitochondria: carbon dioxide, water, and more energy
- in yeast without oxygen: ethanol, carbon dioxide, and less energy
- in muscles with low oxygen: lactic acid and less energy

Activity and figure anchors:
- Activity 5.4: exhaled air turns lime water milky faster

- Activity 5.6: breathing rate increases after exercise
- Activity 5.7: haemoglobin range survey
- Figure 5.7: air passed into lime water

Alveoli have large surface area, thin walls, and rich blood supply for gas exchange. Haemoglobin carries oxygen. Low haemoglobin reduces oxygen transport.

## 5.4 Transportation

Transportation moves materials between specialised organs and body cells.

### 5.4.1 Transportation in human beings

The human circulatory system consists of heart, blood, and blood vessels.

The heart has four chambers:
- right atrium
- right ventricle
- left atrium
- left ventricle

Figure 5.10 shows the human heart. Figure 5.11 shows transport and exchange of oxygen and carbon dioxide.

Double circulation means blood passes through the heart twice in one complete cycle: once through the lungs and once through the body. This keeps oxygen-rich and carbon dioxide-rich blood separate.

Blood vessels:
- arteries carry blood away from the heart and have thick elastic walls
- veins bring blood back and have valves
- capillaries have thin one-cell walls for exchange

More to Know:
Blood pressure is measured using a sphygmomanometer. Platelets help blood clot. Lymph carries absorbed fat and drains excess tissue fluid back into blood.

### 5.4.2 Transportation in plants

Plants use two main conducting tissues:
- xylem transports water and minerals
- phloem transports products of photosynthesis

Figure 5.12 shows water movement during transpiration in a tree.

Activity 5.8 compares a plant and a stick covered with plastic to show transpiration from the plant.

Water movement:
- roots actively take up ions
- water enters root xylem and creates root pressure
- transpiration from leaves creates a pull that lifts water in tall plants

Food movement:
Translocation in phloem moves sucrose and other substances from leaves to storage or growing regions. It uses energy from ATP.

## 5.5 Excretion

Excretion removes metabolic wastes from the body.

### 5.5.1 Excretion in human beings

The human excretory system includes:
- kidneys
- ureters
- urinary bladder
- urethra

Figure 5.13 shows the excretory system. Figure 5.14 shows the nephron.

Nephrons filter blood and form urine:
1. filtration in the glomerulus
2. filtrate collected by Bowman's capsule
3. useful substances selectively reabsorbed in the tubule
4. remaining waste-rich fluid becomes urine

Urine amount depends on excess water in the body and the amount of dissolved waste to be removed.

Artificial kidney:
Dialysis removes nitrogenous wastes through semi-permeable tubes in dialysing fluid. It is similar to kidney filtration but does not perform the same selective reabsorption as a natural nephron.

Organ donation:
The textbook includes organ donation as a life-saving social and medical idea. Treat it respectfully and factually.

### 5.5.2 Excretion in plants

Plants remove or manage wastes by:
- releasing oxygen from photosynthesis
- losing excess water through transpiration
- storing wastes in cellular vacuoles
- storing wastes in leaves that fall off
- storing resins and gums in old xylem
- excreting some substances into surrounding soil

Do not reduce this to only "plants release wastes through stomata"; that misses the textbook's variety of methods.

## exam-friendly summary notes

- Life processes maintain living structures.
- Photosynthesis needs carbon dioxide, water, sunlight, and chlorophyll.
- Salivary amylase digests starch.
- Bile emulsifies fats and makes the medium alkaline.
- Aerobic respiration releases more energy than anaerobic respiration.
- Alveoli maximise gas exchange by large surface area and thin walls.
- Double circulation keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate.
- Xylem carries water and minerals; phloem carries food using ATP.
- Nephron is the functional unit of kidney.
- Plants store, shed, transpire, or release wastes in different ways.

## common mistakes students make

1. Writing breathing and respiration as the same thing.
2. Forgetting Amoeba, Paramoecium, and villi.
3. Saying bile digests fats directly with enzymes.
4. Missing mitochondria in pyruvate breakdown.
5. Saying all arteries carry oxygenated blood.
6. Confusing xylem and phloem.
7. Forgetting Bowman's capsule and selective reabsorption.
8. Giving only one method of plant excretion.

## solved practice set

### question 1
Why is diffusion insufficient in multicellular organisms?

Answer:
All cells are not directly exposed to the environment, so diffusion is too slow to supply oxygen and food or remove wastes from every cell.

### question 2
How are fats digested in our body?

Answer:
Bile emulsifies large fat globules into small droplets in the small intestine. Pancreatic enzymes then digest the fats.

### question 3
How are alveoli designed for gas exchange?

Answer:
Alveoli have large surface area, thin walls, and a rich blood-capillary network.

### question 4
Why is double circulation necessary?

Answer:
It keeps oxygenated and deoxygenated blood separate, giving efficient oxygen supply to the body.

### question 5
Describe nephron function.

Answer:
A nephron filters blood, collects filtrate in Bowman's capsule, selectively reabsorbs useful substances, and sends the remaining waste as urine.

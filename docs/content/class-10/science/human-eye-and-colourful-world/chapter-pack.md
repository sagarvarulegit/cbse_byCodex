# Human Eye and Colourful World

## chapter goal

By the end of this chapter, a student should be able to:
- explain the structure and working of human eye
- understand power of accommodation
- identify defects of vision and their corrections
- explain prism refraction, dispersion, atmospheric refraction, and scattering

## 1) human eye

Important parts:
- cornea: transparent front surface
- iris: controls pupil size
- pupil: opening for light
- eye lens: focuses light
- retina: light-sensitive screen
- optic nerve: carries signals to brain

Image formed on retina is real and inverted. Brain interprets it correctly.

## 2) power of accommodation

Power of accommodation is the ability of the eye lens to change focal length to see nearby and distant objects clearly.

Near point of normal adult eye is about `25 cm`.

Far point is infinity.

## 3) defects of vision

### myopia

Myopia means near-sightedness.

Person can see nearby objects clearly but not distant objects.

Image forms in front of retina.

Correction: concave lens.

### hypermetropia

Hypermetropia means far-sightedness.

Person can see distant objects clearly but not nearby objects.

Image forms behind retina.

Correction: convex lens.

### presbyopia

Presbyopia happens due to ageing and reduced accommodation.

Sometimes bifocal lenses are used.

## 4) refraction through prism and dispersion

A prism bends light due to refraction.

White light splits into seven colours through a prism. This is dispersion.

Colour order:
`VIBGYOR`

Violet deviates most. Red deviates least.

Rainbow forms due to dispersion, refraction, and internal reflection in water droplets.

## 5) atmospheric refraction

Earth's atmosphere has layers of different optical density. This bends light.

Effects:
- twinkling of stars
- stars appear slightly higher
- advanced sunrise
- delayed sunset

Planets usually do not twinkle much because they appear as extended sources.

## 6) scattering of light

Scattering is redirection of light by small particles.

Tyndall effect is scattering of light by colloidal particles.

Sky appears blue because smaller blue wavelengths are scattered more by air molecules.

Sun appears reddish at sunrise and sunset because blue light is scattered away over a long atmospheric path, leaving more red light to reach our eyes.

## exam-friendly summary notes

- Normal near point is `25 cm`.
- Myopia is corrected by concave lens.
- Hypermetropia is corrected by convex lens.
- White light disperses into VIBGYOR.
- Atmospheric refraction causes twinkling.
- Scattering explains blue sky and red sunset.

## common mistakes students make

1. Mixing myopia and hypermetropia corrections.
2. Saying stars twinkle because they produce flickering light.
3. Forgetting violet deviates most in prism.
4. Calling dispersion and scattering the same thing.
5. Saying planets twinkle as strongly as stars.

## board tips

- Draw eye defect diagrams with corrected lens.
- Remember: myopia = concave, hypermetropia = convex.
- In blue sky answers, mention shorter wavelength scattering.
- For sunrise/sunset, mention longer atmospheric path.

## solved practice set

### question 1
Which lens corrects myopia?

Answer:
Concave lens.

### question 2
Why does the sky appear blue?

Answer:
Air molecules scatter shorter wavelength blue light more strongly.

### question 3
Why do stars twinkle?

Answer:
Due to atmospheric refraction through changing layers of air.

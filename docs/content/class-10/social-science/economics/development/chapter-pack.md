# Chapter 1: Development

## Hook

Suppose a new factory creates jobs but pollutes a village pond. Is that development? The answer changes depending on whose life we examine. This chapter gives us a fair way to judge such choices.

## Chapter goal

This chapter helps us understand why people may want different or even conflicting kinds of development. It then shows why income alone cannot measure a good life, how countries and states are compared, why public facilities matter, and why today’s progress must not destroy tomorrow’s resources.

## WHAT DEVELOPMENT PROMISES — DIFFERENT PEOPLE, DIFFERENT GOALS

Development means improvement in life, but the desired improvement depends on a person’s situation. A landless labourer may want more days of work and better wages. A farmer may want a good crop price. A girl may want the same freedom and education as her brother.

Goals can also conflict. A dam may provide electricity and irrigation, yet displace families. We must therefore ask who gains, who loses, and whether the choice is fair.

## INCOME AND OTHER GOALS

Income matters because it buys food, housing, education and other necessities. However, people also value equal treatment, freedom, security, respect, a safe workplace, time for family and a clean environment.

The best job is not always the one with the highest pay. A lower-paid job may offer regular work, safety and learning. Development is therefore a mix of material goals and non-material goals.

People may disagree about what the country should do. National development means discussing which path is fair and just, whether it benefits many people or only a small group, and how conflicting goals can be balanced.

## HOW TO COMPARE DIFFERENT COUNTRIES OR STATES?

For a particular purpose, we choose a suitable criterion. Income is one important criterion because it supports many needs. Total income cannot fairly compare countries of different population sizes, so we use average income.

`Per capita income = total income of the country ÷ total population`

The World Bank uses per capita income to classify countries. This measure is useful, but it does not tell us how income is distributed or how healthy, educated, secure and free people are.

## INCOME AND OTHER CRITERIA

Income is not enough. We also examine health and education. Important measures include Infant Mortality Rate, literacy rate and Net Attendance Ratio.

- Infant Mortality Rate is the number of children who die before the age of one year per 1,000 live births in a year.
- Literacy rate shows the proportion of literate people in the stated age group.
- Net Attendance Ratio shows the percentage of children in a particular age group attending school.

## PUBLIC FACILITIES

Money cannot individually buy every condition needed for a good life. Safe water, sanitation, schools, hospitals and a working Public Distribution System are often provided collectively. Public action can make these services cheaper, more reliable and available to all.

## SUSTAINABILITY OF DEVELOPMENT

Sustainable development meets present needs without reducing the ability of future generations to meet theirs. Renewable resources can be replenished, but they can still be overused. Non-renewable resources exist in a fixed stock and will eventually be exhausted.

The warning “We have not inherited the world from our forefathers—we have borrowed it from our children” reminds us that future people also have a claim on resources.

## EXERCISES: Questions and answers

### 1. Development of a country can generally be determined by (i) its per capita income (ii) its average literacy level (iii) health status of its people (iv) all the above

Answer: (iv) all the above.

### 2. Which of the following neighbouring countries has better performance in terms of human development than India? (i) Bangladesh (ii) Sri Lanka (iii) Nepal (iv) Pakistan

Answer: (ii) Sri Lanka.

### 3. Assume there are four families in a country. The average per capita income of these families is Rs 5000. If the income of three families is Rs 4000, Rs 7000 and Rs 3000 respectively, what is the income of the fourth family? (i) Rs 7500 (ii) Rs 3000 (iii) Rs 2000 (iv) Rs 6000

Answer: (iv) Rs 6000. Total income is `4 × Rs 5000 = Rs 20,000`; the known incomes total Rs 14,000.

### 4. What is the main criterion used by the World Bank in classifying different countries? What are the limitations of this criterion, if any?

Answer: The World Bank mainly uses per capita income. It is easy to compare, but it hides income distribution and does not directly measure health, education, equality, security or freedom.

### 5. In what respects is the criterion used by the UNDP for measuring development different from the one used by the World Bank?

Answer: The World Bank mainly uses per capita income. UNDP’s human-development measure combines income with health and education indicators.

### 6. Why do we use averages? Are there any limitations to their use? Illustrate with your own examples related to development.

Answer: Averages make populations of different sizes comparable. They hide differences within a group. For example, two villages may have the same average income even if one shares income evenly and the other contains one rich family and many poor families.

### 7. Kerala, with lower per capita income has a better human development ranking than Haryana. Hence, per capita income is not a useful criterion at all and should not be used to compare states. Do you agree? Discuss.

Answer: No. Per capita income is useful because income supports many needs, but it is incomplete. It must be combined with health, education, public facilities and distribution of income.

### 8. Find out the present sources of energy that are used by the people in India. What could be the other possibilities fifty years from now?

Answer: Present sources include coal, petroleum, natural gas, hydropower, biomass, nuclear power, solar power and wind power. Fifty years from now, solar, wind, green hydrogen, storage and other cleaner renewable sources may supply a much larger share.

### 9. Why is the issue of sustainability important for development?

Answer: Development depends on resources and a healthy environment. If present use exhausts resources or causes lasting damage, future generations will not be able to meet their needs.

### 10. “The Earth has enough resources to meet the needs of all but not enough to satisfy the greed of even one person”. How is this statement relevant to the disscusion of development? Discuss.

Answer: The statement separates reasonable needs from unlimited consumption. Fair development shares resources and uses them carefully; greed causes inequality, depletion and environmental damage.

### 11. List a few examples of environmental degradation that you may have observed around you.

Answer: Examples include polluted air and water, plastic waste, loss of trees, falling groundwater, soil damage and noise pollution.

### 12. For each of the items given in Table 1.6, find out which country is at the top and which is at the bottom.

Answer: GNI per capita—top Sri Lanka, bottom Nepal; life expectancy—top Sri Lanka, bottom Myanmar; mean years of schooling—top Sri Lanka, bottom Pakistan; HDI rank—best Sri Lanka, lowest Pakistan.

### 13. The following table shows the proportion of adults (aged 15-49 years) whose BMI is below normal (BMI <18.5 kg/m²) in India. It is based on a survey of various states for the year 2019-21. Look at the table and answer the following questions. (i) Compare the nutritional level of people in Kerala and Madhya Pradesh. (ii) Can you guess why around one-fifth of people in the country are undernourished even though it is argued that there is enough food in the country? Describe in your own words.

Answer: (i) Kerala has much lower proportions below normal BMI—8.5% of males and 10% of females—than Madhya Pradesh, where both are 28%. (ii) Enough total food does not guarantee access. Poverty, unequal distribution, weak public services, poor sanitation, illness and gaps in the Public Distribution System can leave people undernourished.

## Common mistakes

- Treating development as “more income” only.
- Saying different goals and conflicting goals mean the same thing.
- Comparing total income when populations differ.
- Assuming an average shows how income is shared.
- Calling every renewable resource unlimited.

## Board tips

- For a comparison answer, write the criterion, the evidence and one limitation.
- When a question says “discuss”, show both sides before giving a reasoned conclusion.
- Use textbook terms such as per capita income, public facilities, Human Development Index and sustainability correctly.
- Quote table values only when they directly support the answer; do not memorise unsupported predictions.

## Answer check

1. Different groups may receive different benefits and costs.
2. Rs 6,000.

4. Renewable resources can be replenished, such as groundwater, though they can be overused. Non-renewable resources have a fixed stock, such as crude oil.

## One-minute revision

- Developmental goals depend on life situations and may conflict.
- Income matters, but dignity, equality, security, health and education matter too.
- Per capita income compares average income and can hide inequality.
- Public facilities turn income into a healthier and better-educated life.
- Human development studies income, health and education together.
- Sustainability protects resources and opportunities for future generations.

# NCERT Exercises and Chapter Revision

This is the final stop on the canonical textbook path. Attempt each question
before checking the answer. A correct option without a reason is not enough
when the question asks you to explain.

## What You Have Learnt

- Acid-base indicators show the presence of acids and bases.
- Acids form `H+(aq)` or `H3O+` ions in solution.
- Bases form `OH-(aq)` ions in solution.
- Acid + metal gives salt + hydrogen.
- Some bases react with some metals to give hydrogen and a salt whose negative
  ion contains the metal and oxygen.
- Acid + metal carbonate or hydrogencarbonate gives salt + carbon dioxide +
  water.
- Aqueous acids and bases conduct because they contain mobile ions.
- The pH scale generally runs from `0` to `14`.
- Neutral pH is `7`; acidic pH is below `7`; basic pH is above `7`.
- Living organisms carry out their activities within suitable pH ranges.
- Mixing concentrated acids or bases with water is highly exothermic.
- Acids and bases neutralise each other to form salt and water.
- Water of crystallisation is a fixed number of water molecules in one formula
  unit of a salt.
- Salts have many everyday and industrial uses.

## NCERT Exercises 1-15

**❓ QUESTIONS**

**1. A solution turns red litmus blue, its pH is likely to be**

**(a) 1  (b) 4  (c) 5  (d) 10**

*Answer:* **(d) 10.** Turning red litmus blue shows that the solution is
basic, so its pH must be above `7`.

**2. A solution reacts with crushed egg-shells to give a gas that turns lime-water milky. The solution contains**

**(a) NaCl  (b) HCl  (c) LiCl  (d) KCl**

*Answer:* **(b) HCl.** Eggshell contains calcium carbonate. An acid reacts
with it to release carbon dioxide, which turns lime water milky.

`CaCO3 + 2HCl -> CaCl2 + H2O + CO2`

**3. 10 mL of a solution of NaOH is found to be completely neutralised by 8 mL of a given solution of HCl. If we take 20 mL of the same solution of NaOH, the amount HCl solution (the same solution as before) required to neutralise it will be**

**(a) 4 mL  (b) 8 mL  (c) 12 mL  (d) 16 mL**

*Answer:* **(d) 16 mL.** Doubling the volume of the same `NaOH` solution
doubles the amount of `NaOH`, so twice the `HCl` volume is needed.

**4. Which one of the following types of medicines is used for treating indigestion?**

**(a) Antibiotic  (b) Analgesic  (c) Antacid  (d) Antiseptic**

*Answer:* **(c) Antacid.** An antacid is a mild base that neutralises excess
stomach acid.

**5. Write word equations and then balanced equations for the reaction taking place when -**

**(a) dilute sulphuric acid reacts with zinc granules.**

*Answer:* Zinc + sulphuric acid -> zinc sulphate + hydrogen.

`Zn(s) + H2SO4(aq) -> ZnSO4(aq) + H2(g)`

**(b) dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with magnesium ribbon.**

*Answer:* Magnesium + hydrochloric acid -> magnesium chloride + hydrogen.

`Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)`

**(c) dilute sulphuric acid reacts with aluminium powder.**

*Answer:* Aluminium + sulphuric acid -> aluminium sulphate + hydrogen.

`2Al(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) -> Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2(g)`

**(d) dilute hydrochloric acid reacts with iron filings.**

*Answer:* Iron + hydrochloric acid -> iron(II) chloride + hydrogen.

`Fe(s) + 2HCl(aq) -> FeCl2(aq) + H2(g)`

**6. Compounds such as alcohols and glucose also contain hydrogen but are not categorised as acids. Describe an Activity to prove it.**

*Answer:* Set up a circuit using a `6 V` battery, bulb, switch and two nails
fixed in a cork over a beaker. Test dilute `HCl` or `H2SO4`; the bulb glows.
Wash the setup and test glucose solution and alcohol solution separately; the
bulb does not glow. Acid solutions conduct because they produce mobile
`H+(aq)`/`H3O+` ions. Glucose and alcohol do not produce these ions in water,
so they are not acids.

**7. Why does distilled water not conduct electricity, whereas rain water does?**

*Answer:* Distilled water contains almost no dissolved ions, so it conducts
very poorly. Rainwater dissolves carbon dioxide and other substances from air,
forming ions in the water. These ions carry electric current.

**8. Why does dry HCL gas not show acidic behaviour in the absence of water?**

*Answer:* `HCl` produces hydrogen or hydronium ions only in the presence of
water. Without water, the ions responsible for acidic behaviour do not form.

**9. Five solutions A,B,C,D and E when tested with universal indicator showed pH as 4,1,11,7 and 9, respectively. Which solution is**

**(a) neutral?**

*Answer:* D, because its pH is `7`.

**(b) strongly alkaline?**

*Answer:* C, because its pH is `11`.

**(c) strongly acidic?**

*Answer:* B, because its pH is `1`.

**(d) weakly acidic?**

*Answer:* A, because its pH is `4`.

**(e) weakly alkaline?**

*Answer:* E, because its pH is `9`.

**Arrange the pH in increasing order of hydrogen-ion concentration.**

*Answer:* Hydrogen-ion concentration increases as pH decreases:

`11 < 9 < 7 < 4 < 1`

Therefore, the solution order is `C < E < D < A < B`.

**10. Equal lengths of magnesium ribbons are taken in test tubes A and B. Hydrochloric acid (HCl) is added to test tube A, while acetic acid (CH3COOH) is added to test tube B. Amount and concentration taken for both the acids are same. In which test tube will the fizzing occur more vigorously and why?**

*Answer:* Fizzing is more vigorous in test tube A. Hydrochloric acid is a
strong acid and produces more `H+` ions than the weak acetic acid of the same
concentration. Magnesium therefore releases hydrogen faster in A.

**11. Fresh milk has a pH of 6. How do you think the pH will change as it turns into curd? Explain your answer.**

*Answer:* The pH decreases below `6`. Bacteria convert milk sugar into lactic
acid, so hydrogen-ion concentration increases.

**12. A milkman adds a very small amount of baking soda to fresh milk.**

**(a) Why does he shift the pH of the fresh milk from 6 to slightly alkaline?**

*Answer:* The slight alkalinity neutralises some acid and delays souring.

**(b) Why does this milk take a long time to set as curd?**

*Answer:* More lactic acid must form before the added base is neutralised and
the milk becomes acidic enough to set as curd.

**13. Plaster of Paris should be stored in a moisture-proof container. Explain why?**

*Answer:* Plaster of Paris absorbs moisture and changes into gypsum, a hard
solid mass. A moisture-proof container prevents it from setting before use.

`CaSO4.1/2H2O + 1 1/2H2O -> CaSO4.2H2O`

**14. What is a neutralisation reaction? Give two examples.**

*Answer:* A neutralisation reaction is a reaction in which an acid and a base
form salt and water.

`HCl + NaOH -> NaCl + H2O`

`H2SO4 + 2KOH -> K2SO4 + 2H2O`

**15. Give two important uses of washing soda and baking soda.**

*Answer:*

Washing soda:

1. It is used as a domestic cleaning agent.
2. It removes permanent hardness of water.

Baking soda:

1. It is used in baking powder to make bread and cake soft and spongy.
2. It is used as an antacid to neutralise excess stomach acid.

Other textbook uses are acceptable when the question asks for any two.

## Group Activity I: Prepare your own indicator

1. Crush beetroot in a mortar.
2. Add enough water to obtain an extract and filter it.
3. Label four test tubes A, B, C and D.
4. Add `2 mL` each of lemon-juice solution, soda water, vinegar and
   baking-soda solution.
5. Add `2-3` drops of beetroot extract to each tube.
6. Record each colour change in a table.

Red-cabbage leaves and coloured petals of Petunia, Hydrangea and Geranium can
also be used to prepare natural indicators.

Conclusion: A natural pigment can show different colours in acidic and basic
media.

## Group Activity II: Preparing a soda-acid fire extinguisher

Caution: This is a teacher-supervised textbook activity. Do not aim the
discharge at a person and do not perform it without suitable supervision.

1. Put `20 mL` sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a wash bottle.
2. Suspend an ignition tube containing dilute sulphuric acid inside it.
3. Close the bottle and tilt it so the liquids mix.
4. Direct the nozzle discharge towards a burning candle.

Carbon dioxide forms and leaves through the nozzle:

The carbon dioxide does not support combustion, so the candle flame goes out.

## Common mistakes

1. Selecting a basic pH below `7`.
2. Writing only formulas in Exercise 5 without balancing the equations.
3. Saying rainwater conducts because pure water is a good conductor.
4. Ordering pH values instead of hydrogen-ion concentration in Exercise 9.
5. Saying acetic acid reacts faster because it is organic.
6. Forgetting the reason in the milk-and-curd questions.
7. Giving washing-soda uses for baking soda or the reverse.

## Board-answer checklist

Before checking a written response, ask:

- Did I answer every subpart?
- Did I name the observation?
- Did I explain the reason using ions, pH or the reaction?
- Is the equation balanced?
- Did I include the unit or volume where it matters?
- Did I use the exact compound name and formula?

## One-minute revision

- Use indicator evidence before drawing a conclusion.
- Use ion formation to explain aqueous acidity and conductivity.
- Use the inverse pH-hydrogen-ion relationship carefully.
- Use complete balanced equations for reaction questions.
- Link each daily-life application to neutralisation or pH.
- Keep baking soda, washing soda, gypsum and Plaster of Paris distinct.

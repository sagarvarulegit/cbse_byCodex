# 7.1-7.4 Coordinate Geometry

A map location becomes a pair of numbers. Once two points have coordinates, Pythagoras' theorem can measure their distance and similarity can divide the line between them.

## 7.1 Introduction

The $x$-coordinate is the abscissa and the $y$-coordinate is the ordinate. A point is written $(x,y)$. This chapter studies the distance between two points and the coordinates of a point dividing a segment in a given ratio.

## 7.2 Distance Formula

For $P(x_1,y_1)$ and $Q(x_2,y_2)$,

$$PQ=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}.$$

Distance is non-negative. The order of subtraction does not matter after squaring, but keep one order consistently.

Distance from the origin is

$$OP=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}.$$

## 7.3 Section Formula

If $P(x,y)$ divides $A(x_1,y_1)$ and $B(x_2,y_2)$ internally in the ratio $m_1:m_2$, meaning $AP:PB=m_1:m_2$, then

$$P\left(\frac{m_1x_2+m_2x_1}{m_1+m_2},\frac{m_1y_2+m_2y_1}{m_1+m_2}\right).$$

The weight beside an endpoint multiplies the opposite endpoint's coordinate. This is a weighted average, not a simple ratio of coordinates.

For the midpoint, $m_1=m_2=1$:

$$M\left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right).$$

## 7.4 Summary

- Distance comes from Pythagoras' theorem.
- Section formula gives an internal weighted average.
- Midpoint is the $1:1$ special case.

## Common mistakes

- pairing $x_1$ with $y_2$ in a distance calculation
- reversing only one difference
- multiplying a ratio weight by the same-side endpoint in the section formula
- using an external-division sign in an internal-division question

## Board tips

- Write the two labelled points before using a formula.
- Keep the square root until the final simplification.
- State the ratio as $AP:PB$ before assigning $m_1:m_2$.

## Quick practice

2. Find the midpoint of $(-4,6)$ and $(8,-2)$.
3. Find the point dividing $(1,2)$ and $(7,8)$ in the ratio $2:1$.

## One-minute revision

Distance squares coordinate differences. Section formula takes a ratio-weighted average.

## Comic recap

- Coordinate pair: "My first value is $x$; my second is $y$."
- Distance: "Square both differences before adding."
- Section point: "The opposite endpoint gets each ratio weight."
- Midpoint: "I am the simple average."

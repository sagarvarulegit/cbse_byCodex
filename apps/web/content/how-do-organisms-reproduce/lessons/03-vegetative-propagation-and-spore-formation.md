# 7.2.5 Vegetative Propagation

Vegetative propagation is asexual reproduction in which a root, stem, or leaf
develops into a new plant under suitable conditions.

Natural examples:

- potato stem tuber—eyes or buds
- Bryophyllum leaf—buds in marginal notches
- roots, stems, or leaves of suitable plants

Artificial methods include **layering**, **grafting**, and stem cuttings. NCERT
links these methods to sugarcane, roses, and grapes.

## Why growers use it

1. Plants may flower and fruit earlier than seed-grown plants.
2. Seedless or poor-seed plants such as banana, orange, rose, and jasmine can be
   propagated.
3. New plants are genetically similar to the parent and retain its desirable
   characteristics.
4. Many plants can be produced quickly.

## Activity 7.5: potato eyes

[[figure:activity-7-5-potato-buds]]

Cut potato pieces so some contain an eye and some do not. Keep them on moist
cotton and compare growth. Only pieces containing viable buds form fresh shoots
and roots.

## Figure 7.5: Bryophyllum

[[figure:figure-7-5-bryophyllum]]

Buds in leaf-margin notches fall to suitable soil, root, and develop into new
plants.

## Activity 7.6: money-plant cuttings

[[figure:activity-7-6-money-plant]]

Compare cuttings that contain at least one node/leaf with stem pieces cut
between two nodes. New roots and leaves grow from pieces with a node; internode
pieces lack the required bud.

## Tissue culture

[[figure:figure-7-tissue-culture]]

1. Remove tissue or cells from a plant's growing tip under sterile conditions.
2. Place them in nutrient medium; rapid division forms a callus.
3. Transfer the callus to a hormone-containing medium for differentiation.
4. Plantlets form and are moved to soil.

This can produce many genetically similar, disease-free plants from one parent
and is widely used for ornamentals.

# 7.2.6 Spore Formation

[[figure:figure-7-6-rhizopus-spores]]

In Rhizopus:

- cottony threads are **hyphae**, not spores
- upright stalks bear blob-like **sporangia**
- each sporangium contains many **spores**
- thick spore walls protect them in unfavourable conditions
- on reaching a moist surface, spores germinate into new mould

## Why spores benefit an organism

They are numerous, light and easily dispersed. Their protective thick walls
survive unfavourable conditions, and each suitable spore can establish a new
individual when moisture becomes available.

## Activity reasoning

### Why does yeast multiply in sugar solution but not plain water?

Sugar provides food/energy, while warmth and water support metabolic activity
and budding.

### Why does Rhizopus not grow on dry bread?

Spores require moisture to germinate and the hyphae require suitable conditions
for growth.

## Common mistakes

1. Calling potato a root; the tuber is a modified stem.
2. Saying every potato piece grows—an eye or bud is required.
3. Saying vegetative offspring have greater variation than seed-grown plants.
4. Calling a sporangium a spore.
5. Omitting sterility, callus, hormones, or soil transfer from tissue culture.

## Remember

`bud present -> vegetative growth possible`

`sporangium -> spores -> moist surface -> new Rhizopus`
